# Build Pictorial Hill Groups As Subordinate Terrain Chains

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a pictorial map needs hills to read as a lower-relief terrain class beside or beyond mountains
**THEN** use smaller rounded forms organized into loose directional groups, extend them from mountain foothills or into independent chains as the terrain requires, and keep their scale and relief marks subordinate to nearby mountain forms when both appear together

## Do
- Use smaller rounded hill forms so the terrain class differs visibly from mountain peaks.
- Let foothills extend high ground outward from mountains while remaining visibly distinct from the mountain forms they accompany.
- Build independent hill groups along loose directional runs when the terrain is not attached to a mountain range.
- Vary group shape and individual hill size enough to avoid a repeated stamp.
- Add small base or relief marks only after the hill groups themselves read.

## Don't
- Do not treat hills as isolated filler bumps when the map needs a coherent terrain system.
- Do not make hill symbols compete with nearby mountains in scale or visual weight.
- Do not require every hill group to follow the same literal curve or group count.
- Do not turn decorative details into the terrain structure itself.

## Checklist
- Hill groups read as a terrain chain or field rather than random singles.
- When mountains and hills appear together, the hills read as the lower-relief class.
- Foothills can extend from mountains without becoming indistinguishable from the mountain range.
- Group direction varies with the terrain rather than repeating one fixed shape.
- Relief marks remain subordinate to the hill silhouettes.

## Notes
Hills can bridge mountains into lower terrain or form their own chains. Their identity comes from relative scale, rounded pictorial shape, grouping, and subordinate relief treatment rather than from a required numeric size ratio or fixed C- or S-shaped arrangement.
