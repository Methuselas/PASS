# Choose Map Lettering Style to Support Culture, Tone, and Legibility

## Pattern Rule
**IF** map text must identify geographic information while also contributing to the culture, period, or tone of the mapped world
**THEN** choose a readable lettering family whose weight, angularity, flow, formality, or ornament supports the intended world character, test representative labels in map context, and reject decorative treatment that prevents the labels from doing their information job

## Do
- Name the intended cultural, period, or tonal character before selecting the lettering treatment.
- Compare lettering families by visible qualities such as weight, angularity, flow, formality, and ornament.
- Use those qualities to reinforce the mapped world rather than merely to make the text look decorative.
- Test representative labels in the map context before applying the treatment broadly.
- Preserve enough legibility that the label remains functional geographic information.

## Don't
- Do not assume one decorative script is inherently appropriate for fantasy or historical maps.
- Do not attach fixed cultural meanings to one sample letterform family as if they were universal.
- Do not sacrifice the label's information function for ornament.
- Do not invent an entire script system from inspiration alone when no construction method has been established.

## Checklist
- The lettering treatment supports a named cultural, period, or tonal intention.
- Representative labels remain readable on the map.
- The text contributes visual character without becoming detached decoration.
- The chosen family can be applied consistently across the labels that share its role.

## Notes
Map lettering has two jobs at once: communicate place information and participate in world character. General Art principles still govern expressive line and shape character, while this specialized owner handles the map-specific tradeoff between semantic labeling and cultural tone.
