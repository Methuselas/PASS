# Construct Pictorial Mountain Lake From Valley Basin and Perspective Shoreline

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a pictorial mountain landscape needs a lake that feels embedded in the terrain rather than placed on top of it
**THEN** site the lake in a low valley where surrounding runoff can gather, draw its shoreline as a flattened shape consistent with the mountain perspective, let nearby peaks overlap portions of the water, and connect several plausible inflows to one main outlet where the basin spills

## Do
- Read the surrounding slopes first and place the basin where water moving downhill from several directions would naturally collect.
- Compress the lake shape in the viewing direction so the shoreline belongs to the same pictorial perspective as the mountains around it.
- Let foreground and side mountains interrupt the shoreline and hide portions of the lake; use occlusion to make the water feel tucked into the valley.
- Feed the basin with several small streams or runoff paths when the terrain supports them, then choose one lower spillway for the main river leaving the lake.
- Use broken contour marks and sparse dots along selected shore sections to show the ground turning down toward the water without outlining every bank equally.
- Add thin, broken wave marks close to the shore only after the basin, shoreline, and river connections already read clearly.
- Keep the shoreline irregular enough to follow the valley walls instead of treating the lake as a generic oval dropped between peaks.

## Don't
- Do not place the lake independently of the surrounding slopes when the terrain should explain why water gathers there.
- Do not draw the shoreline as an upright circle or unsquashed symbol that conflicts with the mountains' pictorial perspective.
- Do not keep every mountain edge clear of the water; avoiding overlap can make the lake read as a flat cutout in front of the terrain.
- Do not give an ordinary closed mountain basin several equal river outlets; choose one main spillway unless a more complex hydrology is deliberately intended.
- Do not use shoreline texture or wave marks to compensate for a lake shape that is poorly seated in the valley.

## Checklist
- The lake occupies a believable low basin relative to the surrounding mountain slopes.
- Its shoreline is compressed consistently with the pictorial perspective of the terrain.
- At least some mountain forms overlap or occlude the water so the lake reads as embedded in depth.
- Inflows can be traced downhill into the basin, and the main outlet leaves from a plausible low spillway.
- Shoreline detail describes terrain contour without becoming a continuous decorative border.
- Wave marks remain subordinate to the lake shape and are concentrated near the shore.

## Notes
The defining move is to treat a mountain lake as terrain-filled water rather than as an independent water symbol. Basin placement explains the hydrology, the flattened shoreline keeps the water in the same pictorial space as the mountains, and overlap makes the valley enclosure visible. Multiple inflows can converge on the lake, while a single lower spillway gives the basin a clear downstream connection. Shoreline contour marks and small wave lines are finishing cues; they reinforce a lake that is already structurally seated in the terrain.
