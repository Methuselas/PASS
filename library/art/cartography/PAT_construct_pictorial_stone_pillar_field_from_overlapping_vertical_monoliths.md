# Construct Pictorial Stone Pillar Field From Overlapping Vertical Monoliths

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a pictorial map needs a field of tall isolated stone pillars to read as layered vertical terrain rather than as ordinary mountains or disconnected towers
**THEN** block a varied overlapping group of pillar masses, break each mass into stacked box-like cliff planes and ledges, taper the formations into the surrounding ground, and integrate vegetation across ledges, valleys, and bases so the whole field reads as one landscape system

## Do
- Place the pillar field with simple vertical masses that vary in height, width, and overlap so depth is established before surface detail.
- Include small peaks, ridges, and ledges in the initial masses so later cliff breaks have structural anchors instead of being pasted onto smooth columns.
- Convert ridges into horizontal cliff breaks and secondary peaks into descending vertical separations, building each pillar from stacked block-like rock masses rather than one uninterrupted shaft.
- Use broken base contours and short terrain transitions to taper the formations into the surrounding landscape instead of ending every pillar at a hard flat foot.
- Keep the outer silhouette stronger than the interior cliff and rock marks when line weight is available, so the main monoliths remain legible against the background.
- Add vegetation on ledges and in valleys between pillars, then carry smaller vegetation into the bases so the stone field connects visually to the surrounding terrain.
- Add hatching, stippling, broken contour marks, or other texture only after the overlapping pillar masses and cliff planes already read clearly.
- When rendering is used, keep light direction consistent across pillar faces, vegetation, and cast shadows so the overlapping vertical masses reinforce the same depth order.

## Don't
- Do not begin by texturing isolated rock columns before the overall field, overlap order, and major heights are established.
- Do not make every pillar the same width, height, spacing, or silhouette; repeated uniform columns weaken the natural terrain read.
- Do not treat the pillars as smooth cylinders when the intended formation depends on box-like cliff faces, ridges, and stepped ledges.
- Do not let every base terminate as a clean horizontal edge disconnected from the ground around it.
- Do not scatter vegetation independently of the rock structure; use ledges, valleys, and bases as attachment zones that help unify the terrain.
- Do not let interior texture become heavier than the silhouettes and major cliff breaks that define the formation.

## Checklist
- The group reads as a field of overlapping vertical monoliths before small texture or color is considered.
- Pillars vary in size and overlap enough to establish foreground and background relationships.
- Major ridges, ledges, and descending breaks divide the pillars into believable stacked rock planes.
- The bases taper or blend into the surrounding terrain rather than ending as repeated flat feet.
- Vegetation is attached to plausible shelves, valleys, and bases and helps connect the pillars into one landscape.
- Line weight, value, or shadow supports the established depth order instead of contradicting it.

## Notes
Stone-pillar terrain is distinguished less by surface texture than by its vertical massing and overlap. The large pillars are placed first as varied silhouettes, then subdivided into cliff ledges and box-like rock planes that make their height believable. Broken bases and vegetation prevent the formations from reading as freestanding architectural columns. Texture and rendering are finishing support; they should clarify the already-established monolith field rather than create it.
