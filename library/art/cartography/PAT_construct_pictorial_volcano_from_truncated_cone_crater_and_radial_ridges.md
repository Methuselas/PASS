# Construct Pictorial Volcano From Truncated Cone, Crater, and Radial Ridges

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a pictorial regional map needs a dormant volcano to read as a volcanic landform rather than as an ordinary mountain peak
**THEN** establish it from a truncated-cone guide, convert that guide into an irregular asymmetric mountain silhouette with a visible crater opening, integrate it with overlapping surrounding mountains, and run primary ridges from the crater down the cone before adding subordinate contour detail and lighting

## Do
- Begin with a simple cone for placement, scale, and perspective, then imagine the top sliced off so the crater plane is established before the final contour is inked.
- Turn the guide into a rocky silhouette rather than tracing a mathematically smooth cone; letting one side of the summit rise higher than the other helps the rim feel irregular and natural.
- Draw the crater mouth as an uneven opening that belongs to the top plane of the cone, then reinforce enough of its inner rim to make the hollow legible.
- Layer smaller mountains in front of and behind the volcano so the dominant cone sits inside a terrain system instead of floating as an isolated symbol.
- Pull the main ridgelines downward from the crater while following the cone's surface; use broken lines so the slopes stay textured instead of mechanically ruled.
- Use broader, more horizontal ridge strokes on the surrounding smaller mountains so their structure remains subordinate to the volcano's radial descent.
- Add secondary contour lines after the major ridges read, and taper the mountain marks into the ground so the landforms feel connected to the landscape.
- When rendering, choose one light direction, block broad shadow masses first, deepen areas hidden behind ridges and inside the crater, and keep any shading on the light side clearly lighter in value.
- Build highlights from broad to small so they reinforce the same planes already established by the ridges and shadows rather than creating a second, conflicting form description.

## Don't
- Do not use the initial cone as the finished outline; a smooth symmetric geometry reads as a diagram rather than rock.
- Do not paste a perfect ellipse onto the summit without adapting it to the broken rim and mountain perspective.
- Do not run every ridge as an equally straight spoke from the crater; the lines should bend and break with the changing surface.
- Do not surround the volcano with mountains that never overlap it; lack of foreground and background layering weakens depth and landscape integration.
- Do not add dense contour texture before the silhouette, crater, and primary ridges already establish the form.
- Do not make the shadow side and light side equally dark; the value structure should preserve a readable lighting hierarchy even when both sides receive some shading.

## Checklist
- A simple truncated-cone construction can still be inferred beneath the final irregular silhouette.
- The crater reads as an opening in the summit plane rather than as a decorative oval on the face of the mountain.
- The volcano is visually dominant while surrounding mountains overlap it from both depth directions where appropriate.
- Primary ridges descend from the crater and follow the cone's form; smaller mountains use a different, more lateral ridge rhythm.
- Secondary contour marks clarify the slopes without overwhelming the major structure.
- The mountain forms taper into the terrain instead of ending as detached symbols.
- Shadows, crater darkness, and highlights agree on one light direction and retain a clear light-side versus shadow-side value difference.

## Notes
The useful construction is the distinction between a volcano and a generic mountain. A volcano begins from a cone whose top has been removed, so both the crater plane and the slope directions derive from that underlying volume. The final drawing should break the geometry with an irregular rim, rocky silhouette, and broken contour marks without losing the cone's logic. Surrounding mountain overlap gives the feature scale and depth, while radial ridges make the central form read as one continuous volcanic mass. Color, digital layers, and specific blend modes are optional implementations; the durable structure is the truncated cone, integrated crater, radial slope organization, and consistent light hierarchy.
