# Construct Top-Down Rough Stone Dungeon Walls From Varied Masonry Courses

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a top-down dungeon map needs walls to read as hand-built rough stone rather than as flat boundary lines or regular cut masonry
**THEN** establish the room or passage footprint first, wrap it with irregular stones whose lengths and joint angles deliberately vary, then add inward stone courses and restrained surface marks so wall thickness and height remain legible around the playable floor

## Do
- Sketch the room and passage shape before resolving masonry, using the grid only as a scale and routing guide rather than forcing every wall into straight equal-width segments.
- Before inking, block representative stone shapes along the wall to establish a varied size family; deliberately change stone length so repetition does not collapse into a mechanical pattern.
- Ink the individual stones with a mix of straighter and curving edges, and vary the angles where neighboring stones meet so the wall reads as uncut masonry.
- Add one or more additional stone rows on the inner face of the wall when the map needs visible wall height or thickness; use the number of rows as a controlled depth cue rather than unlimited decoration.
- Use broken lines, sparse hatching, or small internal marks to suggest rock form after the masonry courses are readable.
- Add small stones, hatching, or stippling outside the wall where needed to make the surrounding ground feel solid and continuous with the structure.
- When tone or color is used, keep the playable interior visually clearer than the surrounding ground and place subtle overlap shadows along the inner wall edge to reinforce height.
- Keep highlights restrained on upper stone surfaces or inner top edges so they clarify form without making the wall unnaturally reflective.

## Don't
- Do not begin by drawing individual rocks before the room and passage footprint is settled.
- Do not make every stone the same length or repeat one joint rhythm around the room; that changes rough masonry into an accidental pattern.
- Do not use perfectly regular rectangular blocks when the intended wall is built from uncut stone.
- Do not add so many inward courses that the top-down perspective becomes confusing or the wall consumes the usable floor area.
- Do not let hatching, cracks, stippling, or color replace the masonry silhouette and course structure.
- Do not make the outside ground lighter or more visually active than the dungeon interior when the map needs the playable space to remain the focus.

## Checklist
- The room or passage footprint remains readable independently of the stone texture.
- Stone lengths and joint angles vary enough to avoid a regular brick-like rhythm.
- Additional inward courses make wall thickness or height clearer without overwhelming the top-down view.
- Surface texture supports individual stone forms without obscuring the wall boundary.
- The ground outside the dungeon visually connects to the wall rather than ending at a clean empty halo.
- Any shadows and highlights reinforce the same wall height and keep the playable interior visually dominant.

## Notes
Rough-stone dungeon walls are built as masonry wrapped around an already-decided dungeon footprint. The key distinction from a generic wall line is the controlled irregularity of individual stones and the use of additional inward courses to imply a wall with thickness and height. Texture, ground treatment, and lighting then strengthen that construction, but they should remain subordinate to the room shape and masonry organization.
