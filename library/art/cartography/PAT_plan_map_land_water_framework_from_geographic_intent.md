# Plan Map Land-Water Framework From Geographic Intent

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a world or regional map's primary arrangement of land and water is still unresolved
**THEN** choose the broad land-water character from the kind of place and inhabited world the map is meant to portray, establish the major land and water masses as a light revisable framework, and judge the whole map before committing local coastline detail

## Do
- Decide the broad geographic character first: a landlocked region, an island-dominated field, a land-heavy map, an ocean-heavy map, or another deliberate relationship.
- Consider how the intended inhabitants and culture relate to that geography when inventing the setting.
- Sketch the major continents or landmasses together with any major islands and lakes lightly enough that their placement and size can still change.
- Judge the overall land-water distribution as part of the map's composition before spending effort on local shoreline detail.
- Use real or historical maps as reference when they help ground an invented arrangement in believable map structure.

## Don't
- Do not treat one sample land-water percentage as a universal target for every map.
- Do not force an ocean-heavy, land-heavy, or landlocked concept toward an arbitrary 50/50 balance merely because equal areas look tidy.
- Do not begin polishing bays, islands, or shoreline texture while the large land-water masses are still poorly placed.
- Do not let local geographic symbols distract from whether the primary land and water relationship fits the world the map is supposed to show.

## Checklist
- The intended broad geographic character can be named before coastline detail is added.
- The major land and water masses read clearly when the map is viewed as a whole.
- Their distribution supports the kind of place and inhabited world being portrayed rather than a memorized percentage.
- The framework remains cheap enough to revise until the large arrangement works.
- Local coastline detail has not been used to disguise an unresolved large-scale composition.

## Notes
Ocean-heavy, balanced, and land-heavy examples are useful comparison points, not fixed formulas. The durable decision is to make the relative amount and placement of land and water serve the intended map before detail hardens the layout. For invented settings, geography can also participate in world character: a dispersed island field implies different possibilities than a mostly landlocked region. Keep that relationship revisable until the whole map reads.
