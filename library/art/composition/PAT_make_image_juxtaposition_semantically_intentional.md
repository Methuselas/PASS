# Make Image Juxtaposition Semantically Intentional

## Pattern Rule
**IF** two or more images are placed close enough in a layout or sequence that viewers will read them in relation to one another
**THEN** decide what relationship their adjacency should imply and control subject, form, scale, time, causality, representation, and contrast so the juxtaposition produces that intended meaning
**ELSE** separate or clarify images when no meaningful relationship should be inferred.

## Do
- State the intended relationship before arranging the images: cause and result, before and after, similarity, opposition, shared form, shared idea, contextual association, or another specific connection.
- Use similarities to invite linkage and differences to create useful tension. Scale, time, subject nature, level of abstraction, and formal structure can all carry the comparison.
- Let representational and abstract images interact when the contrast helps viewers move between direct information and interpretation.
- Use adjacency deliberately when the viewer's act of discovering the relationship is part of the communication; preserve enough evidence that the relationship can actually be inferred.
- Recheck the pair or group as a unit. A strong individual image may still be wrong if its proximity to another image creates an unintended claim, chronology, similarity, or causal reading.

## Don't
- Do not assume neighboring images remain semantically independent just because no caption explicitly connects them.
- Do not create a causal, chronological, or comparative implication accidentally through placement.
- Do not rely on obscurity alone to make a juxtaposition feel sophisticated; if viewers cannot find a defensible relationship, the comparison has failed.
- Do not make every paired image similar in every dimension. Controlled contrast can be the mechanism that makes the intended relationship legible.

## Checklist
- The intended relationship among adjacent images can be stated plainly.
- Similarities and contrasts support that relationship rather than compete with it.
- Any implied chronology, cause, similarity, or opposition is intentional.
- The viewer has enough visual evidence to infer the relationship at the intended level of difficulty.
- The arrangement of the images materially contributes to the intended relationship rather than functioning as incidental decoration.

## Notes
Proximity is interpretive. Once images share a spread, page, panel, or short sequence, viewers tend to search for a relationship between them. That relationship can be literal, such as a process and its result, or conceptual, such as two different subjects linked by a shared idea or contrasted by their nature. Meaning can also arise from differences in scale, time, representation, or form. Use that tendency deliberately: adjacency can communicate, provoke, or create conceptual tension, but accidental adjacency can make claims the designer never intended.
