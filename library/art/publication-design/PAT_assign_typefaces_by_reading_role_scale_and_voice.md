# Assign Typefaces by Reading Role, Scale, and Voice

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a publication needs typography for sustained reading, supporting information, and high-impact display text
**THEN** assign typefaces by role, choosing body faces for comfortable small-size reading and adequate family range, selecting display and support faces for the voice and associations the intended audience should perceive, and making every mixed-face contrast deliberate enough to clarify function or meaning
**ELSE** use a simpler single-family system when one family can satisfy every required role clearly.

## Do
- Evaluate body type at the actual small reading size, not only in a large specimen where fine details are easier to admire.
- Prefer body families with enough useful styles or weights to handle emphasis and supporting hierarchy without forcing unrelated substitutions.
- Use display faces for titles, headlines, logos, or other short high-impact applications where distinctive character can be stronger than in sustained body copy.
- Coordinate body, supporting, and display typography so their differences clarify role while still belonging to the same publication.
- Evaluate a typeface's historical, cultural, and stylistic associations in the intended audience context; treat those associations as audience-dependent signals rather than inherent meanings.
- When mixing families, make the relationship decisive. Closely related forms can support fluid transitions inside running text, while strongly contrasting forms create fixation and clearer separation between information roles.
- Use a limited family set unless additional faces have explicit jobs. Two families are often enough for useful variety, but the governing test is whether every change signifies something to the reader.
- If the primary family lacks a needed weight or style, choose a supplement by comparing width, stroke weight, contrast, terminals, junctures, and other letterform characteristics rather than accepting the nearest nominal weight.
- Let typographic character reinforce the intended atmosphere, positioning, or historical register of the publication without sacrificing the information each role must deliver.
- Within an established family or role, use changes in weight, posture, and width deliberately as voice and cadence controls as well as hierarchy controls. Heavier forms can read as louder or more forceful, lighter forms as quieter, italics as faster or more urgent, and expanded or condensed widths as slower or faster in linear cadence; treat these as tendencies to test in context rather than fixed meanings.
- Recheck spacing when weight or posture changes. Bold strokes reduce counterspace and may tolerate slightly tighter setting, while italics often need additional separation to keep their rhythm from crowding.

## Don't
- Do not choose body type solely because it looks distinctive at headline size.
- Do not use an expressive display face for long reading merely because it gives the publication a strong voice.
- Do not add unrelated typefaces when weight, style, size, or spacing inside the existing family can already establish the needed hierarchy.
- Do not mix subtly different faces without a communicative reason; weak contrast can read as inconsistency rather than hierarchy.
- Do not assume a face's historical or emotional associations will be read identically by every audience.
- Do not assume nominally related styles will preserve the same spacing or cadence when weight, posture, or width changes materially.
- Do not let stylistic novelty outrank readability in any role that carries sustained or essential information.

## Checklist
- Body text remains comfortable and legible at its intended size.
- The selected body family can support the hierarchy the publication actually needs.
- Display typography is readable despite being more expressive.
- Different typographic roles are distinguishable at a glance.
- The combined type system supports one coherent publication voice.
- Every recurring face or style change has an identifiable reading, hierarchy, or semantic job.
- Mixed faces are either deliberately related for continuity or deliberately contrasted for fixation and distinction.
- Audience-specific associations have been checked in context rather than treated as universal.
- Weight, posture, and width changes contribute the intended emphasis or cadence without creating accidental crowding or disproportionate attention.

## Notes
Publication typography works best when type choice begins with function but does not end there. Sustained reading, secondary information, and display text tolerate different degrees of expression and require different performance at scale. Typefaces also carry audience-dependent historical, cultural, and emotional associations, so their selection and combination position the content as well as organize it. A display face can give a publication a memorable voice, but body type must survive repeated reading at small size before stylistic character becomes a virtue. When multiple families are used, their formal relation should be intentional: similarity can support continuous reading, while stronger contrast can create fixation and distinguish information roles. Voice continues inside a family as well: weight, posture, and width alter both visual emphasis and perceived cadence, so style changes should be judged as expressive and spatial changes rather than simple software toggles.
