# Choose Publication Format from Content, Physical Feel, and Production Constraints

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a publication's trim size, binding class, or overall physical format is still open
**THEN** choose the format by balancing content length, intended physical character, expected page count or spine, handling and portability, expected reading circumstances, and the formats the real production route can support
**ELSE** preserve an established format unless changing it solves a concrete reading, production, or physical-use problem.

## Do
- Estimate how the same content changes page count and spine when placed in plausible smaller and larger formats before locking the trim size.
- Judge the object in the hand as well as on screen: a compact thick volume and a larger thin one can carry the same content while feeling materially different.
- Check the actual production route for available trim sizes, binding options, and distribution or manufacturing restrictions before designing around a format that cannot be made.
- When choosing the binding class, test the publication's thickness, durability requirement, need to lie flat, ability to add or remove pages, and the usable area the fastening consumes near the gutter.
- Check whether the chosen construction imposes page-count increments or signature requirements, and resolve those constraints before final pagination rather than padding or cutting pages at the last minute.
- Choose a format whose physical character supports the intended publication rather than selecting size only to minimize pages.
- Test whether the publication must travel easily, fit into an ordinary bag, or support discreet/private handling; let those real carrying and reading conditions influence its physical size.
- Treat standard office-paper proportions and portrait orientation as candidates rather than defaults; test alternate aspect ratios or orientation when they give the publication's words and images a more useful field.
- Treat standard production formats as economically and operationally useful candidates when rack fit, standard sheet use, mailing equipment, or press efficiency matter; because familiar proportions often read as visually neutral, test whether a modest proportion change can add distinction without sacrificing those practical advantages.
- Treat page proportion and overall scale as communication variables as well as containers. Test whether a more compact, expansive, vertical, horizontal, square, narrow, or otherwise unusual field supports the intended character in the actual audience context rather than assigning any proportion a universal meaning.
- When sustained text is the strongest constraint, consider working from the inside out: establish a highly readable text configuration first, then explore page proportions that can house its column structure while still satisfying production and handling requirements.
- Before choosing the page shape for an image-led publication, inventory the dominant image orientations and the space required by captions or explanatory text; let that evidence influence whether portrait, square, or landscape format is the better fit.
- Revisit page architecture after a format change because margins, line length, image scale, and pagination all inherit the new dimensions.

## Don't
- Do not choose trim size as an isolated aesthetic preference without testing what it does to page count, spine, handling, display, and mailing conditions when those conditions matter.
- Do not assume every theoretically desirable size is available from the chosen production method.
- Do not choose binding from appearance alone when thickness, opening behavior, durability, or lost gutter space would make it impractical.
- Do not lock a page count that the chosen folded or single-sheet binding cannot manufacture cleanly.
- Do not force content into an unsuitable standard format merely because it is common.
- Do not let the convenience of a familiar page size decide the publication's aspect ratio before the content has been tested in it.
- Do not size a publication only for shelf presence or visual impact when readers are expected to carry, conceal, or handle it in more constrained circumstances.
- Do not treat a smaller page count as automatically better when the resulting object feels too thin, cramped, or visually cheap for the intended use.

## Checklist
- The selected format can actually be produced through the intended route and fits relevant display or mailing constraints.
- The binding fits the publication's thickness, opening behavior, durability, and gutter needs.
- Page count is compatible with the selected binding or signature structure.
- Page count and approximate spine or physical heft are acceptable for the content.
- The object feels appropriate at the intended hand-held scale and can be carried or handled as the use case requires.
- The format supports rather than fights the page architecture and reading experience.
- The chosen page size and orientation were selected deliberately rather than inherited from a default document or printer-paper setting.
- For image-led work, the selected shape accommodates the dominant reproduction orientations and caption burden without routine cropping or cramped annotation.
- Any change in trim size has been propagated into margins, line length, image scale, and pagination.

## Notes
Format is an early design decision with downstream visual and physical consequences. The same manuscript can become a compact thick book or a larger thinner one, and production services may restrict which combinations are possible. Portability and discretion can also be legitimate design requirements when readers are expected to carry the publication with them or use it privately. Familiar office-paper dimensions are convenient, economical, and often perceptually neutral because readers encounter them constantly, but aspect ratio and orientation still change the field available to words and images. Format can therefore participate in the message: small departures from familiar proportions may create distinction, and extreme scale or proportion can contribute attitude, intimacy, presence, or other effects that must be tested with the intended audience rather than treated as fixed symbolism. A text-led publication may also be built from the inside out, allowing a proven reading measure and typographic architecture to help determine the enclosing page. Binding is part of that same format decision: different constructions change durability, lay-flat behavior, gutter loss, page-count structure, and whether pages can be added or removed. Treat format as part of the publication's character, use, display, mailing, and manufacturing reality, not as a neutral container chosen after the design is finished.
