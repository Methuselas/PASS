# Choose Publication Reproduction Process from Run, Content, Resources, and Time

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a publication can be reproduced through more than one printing or duplication process
**THEN** choose the process by comparing run quantity, content and detail demands, available budget, schedule, personal skill and labor, equipment or vendor access, and the visible or tactile qualities each process can actually deliver
**ELSE** test the available process against the publication's essential content and production constraints before committing the design to it.

## Do
- Compare candidate processes against the actual number of copies required instead of assuming the cheapest-looking setup also has the lowest cost per finished copy.
- Match the reproduction method to the content: test whether small type, fine linework, photographs, full color, tactile impression, or unusual substrates survive the process well enough for the publication's purpose.
- Count setup time and hands-on labor as real production costs when a process requires manual printing, assembly, or repeated handling.
- Check what equipment, local services, remote vendors, materials, and skills are genuinely available before designing around a technique that cannot be executed on schedule.
- Request representative samples or proofs when process quality can vary materially between providers or machines.
- Recalculate the route when quantity changes substantially, because a process that is efficient for a handful of copies can become wasteful for a large run and vice versa.
- When the publisher or producer's own printing, binding, finishing, or material craft is part of the publication's subject or positioning, let the finished production demonstrate that capability through processes the producer can execute reliably rather than leaving the claim entirely in descriptive copy.

## Don't
- Do not choose a printing process from prestige, novelty, or habit without checking whether it fits the run and content.
- Do not assume one process is universally cheaper; setup cost, unit cost, labor, and quantity interact.
- Do not choose a process whose characteristic limitations erase essential small text, fine imagery, folds, or color relationships.
- Do not treat your own time as free when a handmade or labor-intensive method must be repeated across many copies.
- Do not preserve historical quantity thresholds as fixed rules when current vendors, machines, and prices can change the break-even point.
- Do not add premium materials or finishing merely to signal prestige when the process does not support the publication's subject, positioning, or a capability the producer can actually demonstrate.

## Checklist
- Required quantity and schedule are known well enough to compare production routes.
- Essential text, image, color, surface, and folding requirements are compatible with the selected process.
- Available skills, equipment, vendors, and labor are sufficient for the planned run.
- A sample or proof has been reviewed when output quality is provider- or process-sensitive.
- The selected process still makes sense at the current quantity rather than at an earlier estimate.
- When production craft is being used as evidence of capability or positioning, the chosen materials and processes visibly support that claim in the finished object without breaking the practical run, budget, schedule, or content requirements.

## Notes
Different reproduction processes trade setup effort, per-copy cost, fine-detail capability, color behavior, tactile character, equipment access, and manual labor differently. The right choice is therefore project-specific. A short-run photocopied or desktop-printed object, a hand-pulled print, a digitally produced book, and a large conventional press run can each be appropriate when their actual constraints fit the publication. Compare the current project and current production options rather than treating any one technology as the professional default. Reproduction can also carry meaning when the maker's production capability is itself part of the message: stock, binding, embossing, ink, or finishing may act as visible evidence of craft. That communicative use does not cancel the practical test; a process chosen to demonstrate capability still has to fit the run, resources, schedule, and information it must reproduce.
