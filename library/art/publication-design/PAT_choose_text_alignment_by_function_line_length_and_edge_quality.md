# Choose Text Alignment by Function, Line Length, and Edge Quality

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a text block must be aligned inside a publication layout
**THEN** choose justified, flush-left, centered, or flush-right treatment from the text's function and available line length, then inspect the spacing, hyphenation, and visible edge the choice creates
**ELSE** preserve the established alignment when it already serves the text and surrounding hierarchy.

## Do
- Use justification when a dense, economical body block benefits from even left and right edges and the line length is wide enough to avoid disruptive spacing.
- Use flush-left text when natural word spacing and a flexible right edge suit the content or a narrower column better.
- Use centered text selectively for formal, title-like, dedicatory, or similarly short material whose line breaks can be controlled for sense.
- Use flush-right text sparingly for captions, marginal notes, or small supporting units where the right edge helps connect the text to nearby elements.
- Inspect the rag of flush-left text and the word spacing and hyphenation of justified text; alignment is successful only when its resulting edge and internal spacing look intentional.
- Judge a rag by its rhythm across many lines rather than by one unusually short or long line. A visibly active rag can work when its irregularity is consistent enough to stop calling attention to itself.
- If justified text repeatedly produces rivers or if ragged text requires constant hyphenation, adjust measure or type size before forcing line-by-line spacing corrections.

## Don't
- Do not justify narrow columns when the result is rivers, large gaps, or excessive hyphenation.
- Do not center long body copy simply because centered headings look formal.
- Do not accept a flush-left rag that forms distracting flat shelves, repeated word endings, or accidental patterns.
- Do not eliminate every hyphen at the cost of a much worse rag; occasional controlled hyphenation can support a more even edge.
- Do not use flush-right alignment as routine body copy when its irregular starting edge makes sustained reading unnecessarily difficult.

## Checklist
- Alignment fits the text's role rather than being applied uniformly to every element.
- Justified text has controlled spacing and hyphenation.
- Ragged text has a natural, non-distracting edge whose overall rhythm remains stable across representative passages.
- Recurring rivers or excessive hyphenation trigger a measure or type-size check rather than repeated local patching.
- Short centered or flush-right units have deliberate line breaks and clear relationships to nearby elements.
- The chosen alignment supports the page hierarchy and reading flow.

## Notes
Alignment changes both reading behavior and page shape. Justification creates a compact rectangular body but becomes unstable when lines are too short; flush-left protects natural spacing but requires active control of the rag. Centered and flush-right settings are useful accents and relationship tools, not default solutions for sustained text. A useful diagnostic distinction is that the rag, hyphenation rate, and wordspace pattern are outputs of the relationship between alignment, measure, and type size. When those outputs fail repeatedly, repair the underlying relationship rather than treating each line as an isolated exception.
