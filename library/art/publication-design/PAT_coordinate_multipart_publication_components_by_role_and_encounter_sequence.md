# Coordinate Multipart Publication Components by Role and Encounter Sequence

## Pattern Rule
**IF** one publication is delivered as several physical pieces that readers encounter together
**THEN** give each component a distinct communication or use role, arrange their physical nesting and visibility as an intentional encounter sequence, and make the transitions among components carry the reader from identification and context toward action, reference, or concrete evidence
**ELSE** avoid splitting one publication into extra pieces when a single coherent object can serve the same reader need more clearly.

## Do
- Inventory every physical component and state the job each one performs: orientation, editorial context, catalog or reference information, ordering or action, demonstration, sample, enclosure, or another necessary role.
- Map the order in which the audience will actually encounter the pieces when mailed, stacked, wrapped, opened, unfolded, or removed; treat that order as part of the communication rather than as neutral packaging.
- Let different formats or materials reinforce genuinely different roles while preserving enough shared identity that the pieces still read as one publication system.
- Use one component to bridge another when the reader must move from an abstract claim to a concrete result; when physical finish or production quality is itself relevant evidence, a representative sample may serve as a component rather than being simulated only with photography or description.
- When an outer enclosure is transparent, translucent, cut away, or otherwise reveals inner material before opening, compose the visible layers together so the first view already establishes the intended hierarchy and identity.
- Keep the path from contextual material to functional information clear. A supporting editorial piece, instruction or ordering piece, and physical sample may be different objects, but their relationship should be obvious without requiring the reader to infer why they were bundled.
- Test the complete assembled package at actual size, including nesting, removal, refolding, and reassembly, before treating the component system as resolved.

## Don't
- Do not make every component repeat the same message merely to justify a multipart package.
- Do not treat the envelope, wrapper, stacking order, or partially visible inner pieces as design-neutral when the audience sees them before the contents are fully opened.
- Do not add tactile samples, inserts, or ephemera when they increase cost and complexity without demonstrating something the printed explanation cannot communicate adequately.
- Do not let a visually dominant component obscure the practical piece the reader needs for ordering, navigation, instructions, or another required action.
- Do not give related pieces such different identities that the audience cannot tell they belong to one publication.

## Checklist
- Every physical component has a necessary, nonredundant role.
- The actual first-to-last encounter order has been mapped and tested.
- The transition between components clarifies rather than fragments the publication's message or use.
- Shared identity remains visible even when component materials, formats, or visual intensity differ.
- Any physical sample or inserted object demonstrates a quality that matters to the audience rather than functioning as novelty alone.
- The assembled, opened, and reassembled package remains navigable and physically coherent at final size.

## Notes
A multipart publication can communicate through the relationship among objects, not only through the layouts inside each one. One piece may provide editorial context, another may carry dense functional information, and another may supply direct physical evidence. Their order, visibility, material contrast, and nesting can create a conceptual progression from message to use to tangible result. The design problem is therefore not simply to make several attractive pieces, but to make their distinct roles legible as one physical communication sequence.
