# Frame Publication Concept from Subject, Audience, and Positioning

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a publication has content but its visual concept or design brief has not yet been resolved
**THEN** separate the intrinsic subject or message, the audience's need and use context, and any branding or positioning layer, use the intrinsic subject as the anchor for visual direction, and let audience and positioning filter that direction rather than beginning with arbitrary style
**ELSE** preserve the established concept unless a change in content, audience, use, or positioning creates a concrete contradiction.

## Do
- State the publication's primary subject or message in neutral terms before deciding how it should look.
- Identify the intended audience by more than a broad demographic label: establish what information they need, why they need it, how often they need it, how they are likely to receive it, and what they are expected to do with it.
- Distinguish the audience-facing interpretation from a separate branding or positioning message. Treat the latter as an added point of view that can differentiate the publication without becoming confused with the subject itself.
- Break the content into its major parts early enough to reveal whether the subject is continuous, episodic, systematic, concrete, abstract, documentary, experiential, or otherwise structurally distinctive.
- Mine the subject's intrinsic qualities for visual cues. Compare candidate typography, imagery, color, spatial character, and degree of abstraction against what the content actually communicates rather than against a preferred look.
- Compare related publications when positioning matters. Note the visual qualities readers already associate with the subject, then decide which conventions should be preserved for recognition and which may be changed to create a distinct point of view.
- Record the result as a brief that names the subject, content parts, audience and use, information cadence or delivery context, desired interpretive qualities, and any identity constraints that later visual decisions must honor.
- Decide deliberately which conceptual layer should dominate. A subject-forward treatment can feel transparent and informational; an audience-forward treatment can emphasize belonging and resonance; a positioning-forward treatment can make the publication feel more explicitly persuasive or branded.

## Don't
- Do not choose an interesting visual treatment first and retrofit a conceptual explanation after the fact.
- Do not let branding, fashion, or novelty make the subject harder to recognize or understand unless obscurity is itself an intentional part of the communication.
- Do not treat audience identity as a single demographic label when information need, timing, use, and shared context are what actually change the design problem.
- Do not copy the visual language of competing publications merely because it is conventional, and do not reject convention merely to appear different.
- Do not confuse the publisher's preferred interpretation of the subject with the subject's intrinsic content.
- Do not proceed into detailed layout while the brief still leaves the publication's basic message, audience use, or positioning unresolved.

## Checklist
- The primary subject or message can be stated without relying on the proposed visual style.
- The intended audience is defined by a concrete information need, use, timing, or delivery context rather than by demographic shorthand alone.
- Any branding or positioning message is explicit and distinguishable from the intrinsic subject.
- The major content parts are known well enough to expose structural requirements before layout begins.
- The proposed visual qualities can be traced to the subject, audience, or positioning instead of only to designer preference.
- Comparison with related publications has clarified which expectations should be met and which differences are intentional.
- The resulting brief supplies usable constraints for later choices in publication vehicle, format, imagery, typography, color, and page structure.

## Notes
A publication concept carries several simultaneous functions. The first is intrinsic: what the publication is actually about. The second is relational: who needs that material, in what context, at what cadence, and for what use. A third may be interpretive: the publisher's identity, point of view, or desired position relative to competing sources. These layers can reinforce one another, but they should not be collapsed. The subject is the starting source for visual form; audience and positioning filter how that subject is presented. The practical output is not a mood board detached from the content but a brief that lets later design choices be judged against a coherent communication problem. `VAR_samara_encode_concept_in_functional_structure_and_navigation` is the structural-expression branch: when a subject or audience carries a useful systems idea, let real grid, grouping, rule, or navigation behavior echo that idea while still doing functional work; reject the branch when the metaphor would make the publication less legible or merely decorate it with fake interface cues. `VAR_samara_demonstrate_design_product_through_credible_use_contexts` is the specimen/catalog branch: when a designed product is understood through application, let credible intended-use contexts demonstrate its range and keep the product itself more visually important than a fixed house style; reject contexts that look inventive but fail to show how the product is actually meant to work.
