# Mark Paragraph Boundaries with Clear, Noncompeting Cues

## Pattern Rule
**IF** consecutive paragraphs or subsections in running text need visible separation
**THEN** choose a consistent boundary cue—such as first-line indentation, paragraph spacing, a hanging start, or a subhead—and size that cue strongly enough to register without unnecessarily interrupting the continuity of reading
**ELSE** preserve continuous text when the sequence is already unmistakable from a higher-order break or other established structure.

## Do
- Choose paragraph separation in relation to the body text's leading, measure, alignment, and column density rather than applying an arbitrary fixed gap.
- Make a first-line indent visibly distinct from the normal alignment; increase it when loose leading or a wide column makes a shallow indent disappear.
- Use paragraph spacing when a clean vertical break better fits the page, but keep the gap closer to a paragraph boundary than to a section break so the sequence still reads as one body of text.
- Use hanging starts only when their strong visual interruption and extra horizontal space serve the publication rather than becoming a novelty that competes with reading.
- When a paragraph begins with a subhead, make the space above and below the subhead show that it belongs more strongly to the following text than to the preceding paragraph.
- Omit a routine first-line indent when the paragraph's beginning is already obvious from a page, section, or heading break and the extra cue would be redundant.

## Don't
- Do not stack indentation, a full blank line, and a strong subhead merely to prove that a paragraph changed.
- Do not let paragraph spacing become so large that related paragraphs look like separate sections.
- Do not use a subtle indent that disappears inside loose leading or a wide text block.
- Do not use an unconventional hanging treatment everywhere if the reader must repeatedly relearn where the text begins.
- Do not place a subhead spatially closer to the paragraph above than to the paragraph it introduces.

## Checklist
- A reader can identify paragraph starts quickly without stopping to decode the system.
- The chosen boundary cue is consistent across equivalent text roles.
- The amount of space or indentation is perceptible at actual output size.
- Subheads group visually with the text they introduce.
- Section starts and ordinary paragraph starts remain distinguishable from each other.

## Notes
Paragraph separation is a sequencing problem at the scale of running text. Indents preserve a compact column, open paragraph spacing creates a stronger vertical pause, hanging starts add a conspicuous reference point, and subheads can combine navigation with separation. None is automatically superior. The useful choice is the smallest clear cue that preserves the intended continuity and hierarchy of the text.
