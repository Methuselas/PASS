# Match Page Architecture to Content Mode and Physical Use

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a publication page must accommodate primarily text, primarily images, or a deliberate mixture of both
**THEN** choose columns, margins, image scale, caption placement, type scale, and overall density from the dominant content, intended audience and reading mode, intended visual character, expected viewing conditions, and the way the physical page will be read and held
**ELSE** keep the page architecture simple when the content imposes no special spatial or tonal demand.

## Do
- Let text-heavy pages prioritize a stable readable body area and margins that protect the binding and leave practical handling space.
- Let image-led pages respond to the dominant image shapes and the amount of text that must accompany them rather than forcing pictures into a text-first template.
- Decide whether captions belong beside images, beneath them, or on a facing page by balancing image scale, separation, reading continuity, and available space.
- Adjust inner, outer, top, and bottom margins for the actual binding and handling conditions rather than assuming equal margins are always best.
- Keep the page structure consistent enough to orient the reader while allowing content-driven exceptions when an image or section genuinely needs a different treatment.
- Set reproduction scale from what the image must communicate: preserve meaningful relative scale when comparison matters, enlarge a detail when close inspection is the point, or pull back to include surrounding context when environment or installation matters more than object size.
- Across image-led spreads, vary reproduction scale deliberately when size contrast can create useful rhythm, emphasize a detail, or establish a visual conversation among neighboring works; use borders, white space, or background fields when they help keep separate works clearly framed.
- For child-facing picture books, establish the intended age range, whether the book is expected to be read aloud or directly by the child, and the approximate reading duration before deciding how much text and visual material each page or spread should carry.
- Prototype more than one density when whitespace, page count, or perceived formality materially changes the character of the publication; choose the version whose generosity or compactness fits the work rather than defaulting to either maximum economy or maximum luxury.
- Account for expected viewing conditions when they are materially constrained; for example, increase essential type scale or simplify fragile detail when the publication is meant to remain readable in low light rather than only under ideal illumination.

## Don't
- Do not use one page template indiscriminately for text-led and image-led material.
- Do not shrink important images merely to preserve a habitual text arrangement.
- Do not make every reproduction as large as possible by default when relative scale, detail emphasis, or environmental context calls for a different treatment.
- Do not normalize every image to the same footprint merely for uniformity when varied scale would better express hierarchy, rhythm, or relationships among the works.
- Do not let margins become purely decorative if the binding or the reader's hands need that space.
- Do not create so many page exceptions that the publication loses a recognizable underlying system.
- Do not treat "children" as one undifferentiated reading condition when age and read-aloud versus direct-reading use materially change the page's information load.
- Do not equate wide margins and generous whitespace with quality when the resulting page feels pretentious, overformal, or mismatched to the subject.
- Do not approve typography or fine page detail solely under ideal studio lighting when the intended reading environment is materially harsher.

## Checklist
- The page architecture reflects whether text, images, or their relationship is doing the main communication work.
- Text has a stable readable area.
- Images are large enough for the information they must carry, and their scale reflects whether the reader needs comparison, detail, surrounding context, or deliberate rhythmic contrast with neighboring reproductions.
- Captions can be associated with the correct image without confusion.
- Margins serve binding, handling, and visual structure together.
- Page density and whitespace support the intended tone without sacrificing readability or creating an unintended production burden.
- When audience age or read-aloud/direct-reading mode matters, the page's information load has been checked against that intended use rather than a generic picture-book template.
- Essential text and fine information remain readable under the viewing conditions the publication is actually designed for.

## Notes
Text books and picture-led books do not ask the page to solve the same problem. A useful publication system starts from the content mode, intended audience and reading mode, intended character, expected viewing conditions, and physical use, then sets columns, margins, image fields, caption relationships, type scale, and density accordingly. In child-facing picture books, age, read-aloud versus direct reading, and expected reading duration are concrete design inputs rather than generic demographic labels. Economy of space is a tradeoff, not an automatic virtue: compact layouts can save pages but feel cramped or generic, while generous layouts can increase clarity and physical heft yet become costly or visually overformal. In image-led work, reproduction sizes can also create rhythm and relationships across a spread; framing devices such as borders, white space, or background fields can separate works without forcing them into equal-sized boxes. Test the page as a designed tone as well as a container.
