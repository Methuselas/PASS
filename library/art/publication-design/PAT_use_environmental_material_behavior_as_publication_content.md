# Use Environmental Material Behavior as Publication Content

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a publication is meant to be used in physical conditions that can alter the object and that interaction can reinforce the publication's concept
**THEN** select substrate, ink, and construction so the object survives the intended environment while any deliberate material response becomes a meaningful part of the reading experience, then test the effect under the actual use conditions before production is locked
**ELSE** choose materials for ordinary reading, durability, and reproduction needs rather than adding a reactive production effect for novelty.

## Do
- Name the physical condition the publication must withstand before selecting materials; the environment is a design requirement, not a late stress test.
- Separate two jobs that may coexist: material choices may need to protect the object from the environment, and they may also use that environment to reveal or transform information deliberately.
- Choose a substrate whose dimensional stability and surface behavior remain acceptable under the intended exposure rather than assuming ordinary paper will behave normally.
- When a reactive ink or other changing print effect is part of the concept, make its trigger meaningfully connected to the actual reading situation so the physical response deepens the subject instead of functioning as an unrelated trick.
- Route the selected material and effect through the real reproduction process early enough to confirm that the printer, substrate, ink, and finishing method can work together.
- Build and handle a representative physical sample in the intended conditions, checking both survival of the object and the timing, visibility, and repeatability of the intended material response.
- Revisit the concept, process, or material specification if the physical behavior is unreliable, damages the publication, or distracts from the information it was supposed to strengthen.

## Don't
- Do not treat an unusual stock or reactive ink as self-justifying merely because it is memorable.
- Do not design an environment-dependent effect without confirming that the intended environment actually produces the required physical response.
- Do not let a conceptually clever effect make the object unusable in the conditions where readers are expected to handle it.
- Do not approve environmental performance from a screen preview or an ordinary-room proof when the material behavior depends on conditions the proof never experiences.
- Do not preserve a special process after testing shows that the material response is inconsistent or undermines legibility.

## Checklist
- The intended physical environment and the stress it places on the publication are explicit.
- The chosen substrate and construction remain usable under that environment.
- Any deliberate material response is conceptually connected to the reading situation rather than added as novelty.
- The actual reproduction route can produce the chosen stock, ink, or material combination reliably.
- A representative physical sample has been tested under the conditions that are supposed to trigger or challenge it.
- The resulting physical behavior strengthens the experience without compromising the publication's essential readability or handling.

## Notes
A publication's physical environment can be more than a durability constraint. When the reading situation itself changes the printed object, material behavior can become content: a substrate can be chosen because it remains stable where ordinary paper would fail, while a responsive ink can make information appear only under the same condition that defines the subject and setting of use. The useful move is not “use a special ink.” It is to connect material performance, environmental trigger, and concept so the object's behavior belongs to the experience, then verify that behavior physically rather than assuming it from specifications.
