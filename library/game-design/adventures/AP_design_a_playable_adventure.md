# Design a Playable Adventure

## Objective
Turn a scoped premise into an executable adventure whose entry condition, progression, information access, encounters, risks, outcome states, and climax can respond to player decisions without depending on hidden author knowledge or one prescribed route through play.

## Steps / Flow
1. **Enter with a bounded adventure purpose.** Know the intended participants, the situation or problem that makes this adventure worth playing, the approximate scope of play, and the larger game assumptions it may rely on. If those are still undefined, return to foundation or campaign design rather than using encounter construction to discover the adventure's purpose accidentally.
2. **Establish the minimum shared entry condition.** Use **Establish Adventure Entry Premise Without Prescribing Personal Motivation** to put the protagonists into a common situation, obligation, assignment, location, relationship, or other participation premise. Preserve player ownership of character-specific motives and reactions wherever the premise does not genuinely require them.
3. **Define progression before scripting scenes.** If the adventure needs authored direction, use **Structure Adventure Progression with Milestones and Triggered Beats** to identify required milestones, optional or conditional beats, their triggers, and the ways player decisions can change route and state. If the adventure is intentionally situation-driven without mandatory milestones, record the live situation and skip structural requirements the design does not need.
4. **Prepare executable stopping and continuation states.** Use **Prepare Executable Adventure Outcome States** to define recognizable resolution, continuation, and redirected-failure outcomes with trigger conditions and consequences. Do not make one intended victory the only state the facilitator knows how to run forward from.
5. **Constrain only as much as the supported scope requires.** If authored scope must be protected, use **Use Least-Force Constraints to Preserve Directed Adventure Scope**. Prefer information, incentives, environmental pressure, causal limits, and other lighter constraints before hard boundaries or forced transitions; if a hard boundary is genuinely part of the premise, make it legible rather than disguising it as choice.
6. **Build the information architecture around access routes.** Use **Layer Adventure Information by How Players Can Access It** to separate what is directly observable, discoverable through action, learnable through people or records, and known only to the facilitator. For any fact required for progress, also use **Keep Progress-Critical Information from Becoming a Single Failure Point** so one missed roll, one ignored NPC, or one bypassed location cannot silently halt the adventure. Use **Match Information Precision to Decision Precision** so clues and descriptions are specific enough for the decisions they are supposed to support without over-answering questions play has not earned.
7. **Design each encounter around its actual job.** For every encounter or challenge, use **Calibrate Encounter Challenge to Purpose and Context** to decide what degree of danger, uncertainty, cost, or pressure fits its role in the adventure rather than equalizing all scenes. Then use **Define Encounter Response Space by Intended Challenge** to decide which approaches the challenge is meant to test and which alternate responses should remain viable. Use **Ground Encounter Elements in the Fictional Situation** so terrain, objectives, opposition, resources, and constraints arise from the established situation rather than existing only to satisfy abstract mechanics.
8. **Give severe risks a learnable basis.** Whenever an encounter can impose unusually high, irreversible, or campaign-shaping consequences, use **Give High-Consequence Risks a Learnable Information Basis** so players can obtain enough evidence to recognize, avoid, mitigate, or deliberately accept the danger. If the consequence is ordinary and already legible from the shared rules, do not manufacture extra warning procedure.
9. **Shape challenge across the whole adventure.** Use **Shape Adventure Challenge Progression Deliberately** to place safer areas, pressure spikes, recovery opportunities, optional extreme threats, and situations best deferred or bypassed according to the intended experience. Revisit individual encounter calibration if the aggregate sequence produces monotony, accidental attrition, or a difficulty curve the adventure did not intend.
10. **Earn emotional stakes before threatening them when stake weight depends on attachment.** If the adventure wants loss to matter because players care about a person, place, status, resource, or relationship, use **Build Stake Weight Through Player Investment Before Threatening It**. If the stake already carries established weight from earlier play, preserve that history rather than rebuilding attachment from zero.
11. **Make the climax consume accumulated state.** If the adventure has a climax, use **Make Climaxes Converge Accumulated Play State** so prior relationships, information, resources, losses, choices, and changed conditions can affect how the climax begins or resolves. Do not erase the adventure's preceding state merely to restore a prewritten final scene.
12. **Run the execution gate against the routes the design actually promises.** Walk or playtest the adventure from entry through materially different routes when the structure offers them. For a deliberately narrow structure, test the intended route plus a reasonable deviation, failed expected beat, or bypass where the design permits one rather than inventing a second route solely for audit coverage. Verify that information can still be acquired as required, encounters can be adjudicated, consequences persist, and the facilitator knows what state follows. Any stall routes back to the Pattern owner responsible for the missing information, trigger, response space, or outcome rather than being patched by invisible facilitator rescue.
13. **Close when the adventure can survive player authorship.** Completion requires an executable entry, sufficient progression or live-situation structure, accessible decision-relevant information, grounded and purpose-calibrated challenges, support for consequential risk, runnable outcome states, and—where present—a climax that reflects accumulated play. The adventure need not predict every action; it must remain operable when players make reasonable choices the author did not script.

## Notes
This protocol coordinates adventure-level decisions; it does not replace the Pattern logic that owns them. Directed milestones, high-consequence telegraphing, emotional investment, and a formal climax are conditional branches. A sandbox can complete the action with situation state and outcome support instead of mandatory beats, while a tightly directed scenario can still preserve agency by constraining scope honestly and letting player decisions change the state in which milestones are reached.
