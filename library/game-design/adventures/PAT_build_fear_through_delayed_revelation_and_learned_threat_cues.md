# Build Fear Through Delayed Revelation and Learned Threat Cues

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an adventure needs fear or suspense to come from anticipation rather than only from the revealed threat
**THEN** delay full revelation, feed players concrete sensory or situational cues they can associate with danger, and vary the learned cue-to-payoff pattern deliberately after it becomes recognizable
**ELSE** reveal the threat directly enough for the intended play when uncertainty would add little beyond delay.

## Do
- Keep the threatening unknown partially concealed while characters can still hear, smell, feel, infer, or glimpse enough evidence to imagine what may be present.
- Build tension by adding successive cues before resolving the uncertainty instead of naming the danger immediately.
- Let repeated cues acquire meaning through experience so a sound, visual sign, environmental change, or other recurring signal can create anticipation before the threat appears.
- Use both short tension sequences inside a scene and longer recurring patterns across scenes when each repetition strengthens the association.
- Once a warning pattern is established, vary it purposefully: delay the expected payoff, substitute a lesser or different reveal, or occasionally let the threat arrive without the familiar cue.
- Preserve enough consistency that players can actually learn the pattern before exploiting exceptions to it.
- Let the environment carry threat cues when appropriate, such as recurring noises, weather changes, motion, odor, or other sensory changes that become meaningful through repetition.

## Don't
- Reveal the monster, trap, or other threat at the first opportunity when the intended fear depends on what players cannot yet confirm.
- Substitute vague adjectives such as "scary" or "terrifying" for concrete cues that give players something to anticipate.
- Change the cue-to-payoff relationship randomly before a recognizable pattern exists; without learning, broken expectation becomes noise rather than suspense.
- Repeat the exact same warning and payoff so mechanically that anticipation becomes certainty without escalation, variation, or consequence.
- Use false alarms or omitted warnings so often that players rationally stop treating the established cue as meaningful.
- Confuse suspense with informational unfairness when players need actionable warning for a high-consequence decision; fear design does not replace risk telegraphing.

## Checklist
- The threat remains at least partly unknown long enough for anticipation to develop.
- Players receive concrete cues before at least some threat payoffs rather than only abstract statements that something is frightening.
- A recurring cue is repeated enough to form a recognizable association before the design varies or breaks it.
- Variations in the pattern have a deliberate tension function such as delay, false relief, redirected expectation, or surprise.
- Short scene-level buildup and longer recurring buildup do not merely duplicate each other without increasing anticipation.
- Any high-consequence decision that depends on warning still has an adequate information basis independent of the desire to surprise.

## Notes
Fear can be generated before a threat is visible. A closed door, darkness, obscuring terrain, an unseen source of sound, or any other barrier to certainty gives players room to imagine what they cannot yet confirm. Concrete sensory evidence keeps that uncertainty active while delay lets anticipation accumulate.

A recurring cue can also train expectation. Once players have learned that a particular signal precedes danger, the signal itself carries tension. The design can then alter the established relationship: a cue can lead to a lesser reveal, the expected threat can be delayed, or a later threat can arrive without the familiar warning. These variations work because the pattern was first made legible. This Pattern owns the emotional use of anticipation and learned threat cues; informational fairness, general pacing, and deception remain separate decisions.
