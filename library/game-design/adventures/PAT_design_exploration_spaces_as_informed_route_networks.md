# Design Exploration Spaces as Informed Route Networks

## Pattern Rule
**IF** navigating a location is meant to be a meaningful part of play
**THEN** design its traversable topology as a network of materially different routes and make enough route information learnable that players can choose, retreat, re-enter, and exploit geography deliberately
**ELSE** use a simpler or more linear spatial structure when navigation is only connective tissue or deliberate sequencing is the point.

## Do
- Judge the map by its connection graph, not by how visually twisty or irregular it looks.
- Use loops, alternate entrances, shortcuts, secret or conditional connections, and multiple vertical links when they create distinct approaches, escape routes, flanking opportunities, bypasses, or re-entry strategies.
- Make route choices differ in something consequential such as danger, travel time, visibility, noise, resource cost, faction exposure, accessibility, information, terrain, or reward opportunity.
- Broadcast enough information before commitment for players to distinguish routes through clues such as sound, smell, tracks, architecture, traffic, light, warnings, landmarks, known maps, or prior reconnaissance.
- Place memorable landmarks or other orientation anchors where complexity would otherwise turn navigation into arbitrary confusion.
- Let learned geography become a reusable asset: opened shortcuts, discovered exits, mapped loops, safe routes, and known hazards should improve later movement through the site.
- Use constrained or linear segments intentionally when a sequence needs to teach a local rule, establish a clue, create a chokepoint, or produce another specific spatial effect.
- Check multi-level sites for more than one useful connection between important strata when a single connector would collapse the larger network into one mandatory chokepoint.

## Don't
- Treat a branching corridor whose branches only terminate and force backtracking as equivalent to a genuinely connected route network.
- Add alternate routes that have no meaningful difference in cost, risk, information, opportunity, or access.
- Make every useful connection secret or unknowable so route choice exists on the referee map but not in player decisions.
- Overconnect the site until every area is equally reachable from every other area and route planning stops mattering.
- Require one entrance, staircase, bridge, elevator, or corridor to carry all progression unless controlling that bottleneck is itself the intended decision.
- Confuse spatial complexity with navigational value; a maze can be complicated while still offering few meaningful choices.

## Checklist
- The topology contains at least one meaningful choice of route when navigation is part of the intended play.
- Important alternate routes create different consequences or opportunities rather than cosmetic variation.
- Players can obtain information that distinguishes at least the major route choices before or during commitment.
- Retreat and re-entry can use learned geography rather than always retracing one mandatory path.
- Major vertical or regional transitions do not accidentally collapse the whole site into a single connector unless that is intentional.
- Landmarks, scale, orientation, or other cues are sufficient for the intended navigation burden.
- Any intentionally linear section has a reason to be linear.

## Notes
A spatial choice only becomes a game decision when routes create different futures. Loops and multiple connections are valuable because they let players route around threats, approach problems from another side, retreat through known ground, or convert reconnaissance into advantage. The same techniques become noise when every path is equivalent or when players have no basis for choosing among them. Topology and information therefore work together: build distinct routes, then expose enough evidence for players to reason about those routes.
