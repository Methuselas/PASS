# Design Occupied Sites Around Inhabitant Operations

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an adventure site was built, adapted, or currently inhabited for ongoing use
**THEN** derive its topology, support spaces, traffic, security, and defender placement from what its builders and occupants need to do there before dividing the site into encounters
**ELSE** let abandoned, ritual, anomalous, or deliberately hostile spaces violate ordinary operational logic for a stated reason.

## Do
- Identify both the site's original purpose and its current use when later inhabitants have repurposed older architecture.
- Trace ordinary routes for entry, work, rest, storage, command, supply, waste, maintenance, evacuation, and other recurring functions that matter to play.
- Provide necessary support such as water, food access, ventilation, sanitation, storage, workshops, sleeping areas, or transport only to the degree that the site's operation and player decisions depend on them.
- Place defenses where occupants can actually use, maintain, bypass, reinforce, and retreat through them.
- Let alternative entrances, posterns, service passages, escape routes, portals, and hidden access serve inhabitant needs as well as player route choice.
- Deploy guards and other opposition according to duties, response plans, communication, defensible positions, and available numbers rather than equal encounter-sized packets.
- Use modifications, blocked passages, improvised defenses, wear, refuse, supply trails, and other operational traces as clues to how the site is currently used.
- Break realism deliberately when supernatural construction, alien priorities, ceremonial symbolism, madness, decay, or another fictional cause makes the exception playable.

## Don't
- Design a lair as a chain of rooms whose primary purpose is spacing opposition into convenient fight sizes.
- Make inhabitants repeatedly traverse their own lethal traps, arbitrary riddles, or circuitous routes without a reason or safe operating procedure.
- Give an occupied site no plausible route for essential supplies, waste, access, or evacuation when those omissions would be obvious in play.
- Assume a secondary entrance must be easier than the primary one; it can trade visibility, access, hazard, secrecy, or defensive strength differently.
- Simulate plumbing, airflow, agriculture, or domestic detail exhaustively when those systems will not affect play.
- Place every available defender in a fixed room if the fiction gives them a reason and ability to concentrate, patrol, reinforce, or withdraw.

## Checklist
- The site's architecture can be explained by original construction, current use, or a deliberate exception.
- Important occupants can reach the places they routinely use without absurd self-imposed obstacles.
- Essential operational needs that affect play have plausible support or an explicit supernatural substitute.
- Security features protect something and can be operated by the people who depend on them.
- Defender placement follows roles and response logic rather than only encounter budgeting.
- Alternate access routes have an inhabitant-side reason or a credible history.
- Operational details produce clues, choices, vulnerabilities, or consequences rather than decorative simulation alone.

## Notes
A site becomes more credible and more playable when its geometry belongs to the people who use it. Barracks near a gate, a service route that bypasses ceremonial halls, a protected postern, a water source, or a defensible rally point can all create exploration choices without being inserted solely as game obstacles. This does not require architectural simulation. It requires enough operational causality that players can reason from the space, exploit its routines, and predict how inhabitants may respond.
