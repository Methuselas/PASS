# Give High-Consequence Risks a Learnable Information Basis

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a severe consequence is supposed to be avoidable or mitigable through recognizing danger
**THEN** give attentive play a meaningful basis for informed caution through premise-level warning, learned challenge grammar, recurring patterns, environmental evidence, prior consequences, immediate clues, or another reliable information channel
**ELSE** do not add telegraphing solely to protect players from a consequence whose intended challenge does not depend on prior recognition.

## Do
- Identify what information would let players distinguish ordinary danger from a risk that deserves unusual caution or preparation.
- Distribute that information through the channel that fits the challenge: campaign premise, repeated patterns, environmental evidence, prior consequences, NPC knowledge, equipment feedback, immediate local clues, or another established signal.
- Let players learn a risk grammar over time when recognizing recurring patterns is part of the challenge.
- Make severe danger legible early enough that recognition can still change a meaningful decision.
- Preserve surprise when surprise is the intended challenge, but distinguish surprising content from consequences that depend on withholding information reasonable characters or attentive players should have had.
- Check the whole information environment before adding another warning; a danger can be fair without an isolated sign immediately beside it.

## Don't
- Hide a severe, automatically perceptible threat until after players commit to a choice that reasonable characters would have recognized as dangerous.
- Assume every lethal or severe threat requires an explicit local warning when the adventure has already taught a reliable risk grammar.
- Treat a general statement that "the adventure is dangerous" as sufficient when the intended challenge actually depends on recognizing specific recurring cues.
- Remove every possibility of misjudgment; the goal is a meaningful basis for caution, not guaranteed correct interpretation.
- Use withheld information to manufacture difficulty when the challenge is supposed to be choosing a response to known danger.

## Checklist
- Each high-consequence risk that depends on recognition has at least one identifiable information channel.
- The relevant signal arrives before the decision it is meant to influence.
- The adventure distinguishes global risk grammar from local telegraphing rather than assuming either is always mandatory.
- Attentive players can connect at least some severe risks to evidence, prior learning, or established premise instead of pure authorial surprise.
- Surprise remains where intended without requiring characters to ignore automatically perceptible facts.

## Notes
High consequence increases the importance of informational fairness, but fairness does not require a warning label beside every hazard. *Tomb of Horrors* illustrates the distinction: the expedition is framed as exceptionally lethal, early play teaches probing and careful examination, deceptive architectural patterns recur, and later challenges rely on players learning from that grammar. Local telegraphing is one information channel among several. The reusable test is whether attentive players had a meaningful basis for caution before the consequential commitment, not whether every danger repeated the same warning format.
