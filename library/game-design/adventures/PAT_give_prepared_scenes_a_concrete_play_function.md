# Give Prepared Scenes a Concrete Play Function

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an authored scene is expected to consume meaningful table time
**THEN** give it a concrete play function such as enabling a decision, revealing information, changing state, creating preparation, establishing relationship, providing exploration, or presenting conflict, and compress or combine scenes whose separate existence adds no meaningful function
**ELSE** handle connective travel, empty space, routine procedure, and other non-decisions as transitions unless the table itself turns them into play.

## Do
- Define what the scene lets players learn, decide, obtain, risk, change, prepare, express, or overcome before detailing its surface content.
- Distinguish a **scene** from a **location**. One location can host several scenes over time, while moving through several locations can remain one continuous scene when the same action is still being resolved.
- Let encounters be one kind of scene rather than treating combat, social conflict, or dice use as the definition of a scene.
- Combine compatible functions when doing so produces denser play: a route choice can also reveal setting information; a negotiation can also create an ally; preparation can also expose a risk.
- Leave room for player-created recap, planning, and character interaction when the table needs them even when those scenes were not preauthored.
- Use short transitions to bridge meaningful scenes instead of repeatedly asking players to reconfirm an already settled action.
- When outlining an adventure from likely hurdles, include only obstacles or developments that materially affect progress toward, away from, or around the adventure's unresolved problem.

## Don't
- Number every room, road segment, meal, or conversation as a full scene merely because it exists in the fiction.
- Add a fight, puzzle, or NPC interaction solely to satisfy a content quota when it has no distinct effect on play.
- Define scene purpose as the vague instruction to "move the story forward" when you cannot state what changes for the players.
- Interrupt player planning, recap, or character interaction simply because it was not on the outline.
- Preserve two separate scenes when their functions can be combined without losing a meaningful decision, pacing beat, or state transition.

## Checklist
- Each prepared scene can be described by the player-facing function it performs.
- Locations and scenes are not assumed to be one-to-one.
- Routine transitions do not consume the same presentation weight as meaningful scenes.
- At least the major scenes change information, options, resources, relationships, risk, position, or another actionable state.
- Repetitive scenes with identical functions have been combined, varied, or removed unless repetition itself is intentional.
- The outline can tolerate player-created scenes and skipped potential scenes without breaking unrelated material.

## Notes
Scenes are units of active play, not map labels. Their design value comes from what players can do with them: investigate, decide, prepare, negotiate, discover, express character, confront opposition, or change the situation. This complements encounter-purpose calibration by applying the same discipline to non-encounter play. It also provides a useful compression test for adventure manuscripts: if a separate scene does no work that another scene or transition cannot do, it is a candidate for removal or combination.
