# Ground Encounter Elements in the Fictional Situation

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an encounter contains inhabitants, hazards, secrets, rewards, terrain, or other interactive elements
**THEN** give those elements enough motive, placement logic, state, and local relationship to the situation that their behavior and consequences can be inferred during play
**ELSE** do not stock the encounter with disconnected obstacles or rewards whose only explanation is that the design wanted another challenge.

## Do
- Place inhabitants where their goals, needs, duties, territory, resources, or relationships make their presence useful and understandable.
- For roaming or randomly selected inhabitants, establish enough local context to answer where they came from, where they are going, what they are doing, or what nearby faction, route, settlement, hazard, or resource explains their presence.
- Give hazards a cause, trigger, footprint, or environmental relationship that can inform player decisions when discovered.
- Place rewards and useful resources where ownership, history, security, scarcity, or practical use explains why they are there.
- When an opponent already possesses equipment or another useful asset that could affect the encounter, let that asset affect what the opponent can do before it becomes a reward unless the fiction explains why it is unavailable or unused.
- Let local motives and conditions change behavior when players negotiate, withdraw, alter the environment, remove a leader, expose a secret, or change another relevant state.
- Let inhabitants who know the area exploit established terrain, hazards, equipment, or complementary allies when their competence and motives support doing so; make tactical combinations consequences of the local situation rather than detached combo puzzles.
- Use only enough world detail to support decisions the facilitator may actually need to make during the encounter.
- Check that secrets, clues, hazards, inhabitants, and rewards can interact coherently rather than existing as independent keyed entries with no shared state.

## Don't
- Place monsters, traps, treasure, or other encounter elements without enough in-world reason to explain behavior that matters at the table.
- Write extensive ecology or history that never affects a decision, clue, reaction, consequence, or state change.
- Give every inhabitant the same behavior regardless of motive, losses, negotiation, or changed local conditions.
- Treat rewards as abstract compensation when ownership, legality, portability, scarcity, or local consequences should matter to the encounter.
- Generate useful post-encounter equipment that the defeated owner inexplicably failed to use when it would have materially changed the established situation.
- Assemble enemies into a mechanical synergy that has no credible local relationship, awareness, or reason to coordinate.

## Checklist
- Important inhabitants have motives or local roles sufficient to guide behavior beyond a scripted first action.
- Hazards have enough causal structure to support warning, avoidance, manipulation, or consequence when those interactions matter.
- Rewards and secrets fit their placement well enough to create coherent ownership or discovery consequences.
- Useful assets carried by opposition were available to affect their behavior unless a stated constraint prevented it.
- Local state changes can alter encounter behavior where the fiction says they should.
- Added setting detail earns its cost by informing an encounter decision or consequence.
- Interactive elements can coexist in one situation without requiring unrelated assumptions for each keyed entry.
- Tactical use of terrain, hazards, or allied abilities follows from inhabitants' established knowledge, motives, and relationships.
- Random or roaming encounters can be connected to the surrounding world with at least one actionable origin, destination, activity, or local relationship when that context matters.

## Notes
Encounter coherence is not a demand for exhaustive simulation. It is a demand for enough causality that the facilitator can answer the next reasonable question. Why is this opponent here? What happens if the alarm is disabled? Who owns the reward? Why does the hazard affect this area? When the answers connect to the fictional situation, players can investigate and manipulate the encounter instead of merely discovering a sequence of isolated designer-authored obstacles.
