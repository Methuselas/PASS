# Make Misdirection Playable Rather Than Arbitrary

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an adventure intentionally supports a false belief or misleading inference
**THEN** give the deception a concrete play function and preserve meaningful ways for players to interact with, test, reinterpret, or later understand it without making all information equally untrustworthy
**ELSE** present ordinary information directly enough that players can use it as a stable basis for decisions.

## Do
- Decide what the false belief is meant to create: uncertainty, investigation, altered priorities, social tension, delayed recognition, tactical risk, or another specific play effect.
- Give the deception a causal basis when the scenario benefits from one, such as incomplete evidence, misleading appearance, a motivated actor, altered perception, conflicting testimony, or a deliberately deceptive presentation technique.
- Preserve meaningful interaction with the deception through contradiction, questioning, testing, investigation, learned patterns, later revelation, or consequences that let players update their model.
- Give high-consequence commitments enough basis for challenge when the intended test is whether players recognize or investigate the misleading inference rather than whether they can read the author's mind.
- Maintain a sufficiently trustworthy surrounding information environment that selective suspicion remains useful. Ordinary descriptions, clues, and NPC statements need not all carry the same chance of deception.
- Let players benefit from learning recurring challenge grammar. Recognizing a previously learned trick can be successful play rather than a reason to secretly change the answer.
- Allow direct facilitator deception when it genuinely serves the intended experience, but establish later understanding, consequences, or interaction so the technique does not reduce player decisions to arbitrary guessing.

## Don't
- Treat concealment and misdirection as the same problem. Information can be hidden without actively supporting a false inference.
- Make every clue, object, room, or speaker deceptive until exhaustive distrust becomes the only rational strategy.
- Change the solution after players correctly identify the deception merely to stay ahead of them.
- Use facilitator authority to force a false belief when no later interaction, contradiction, consequence, or revelation can make the deception meaningful.
- Hide every avenue of challenge before an irreversible high-consequence commitment when recognition is supposed to be part of the challenge.
- Dictate what a protagonist believes or feels merely because the scenario presents misleading information.

## Checklist
- The intended false belief has a named play function beyond surprising the table.
- Players have at least one meaningful way to interact with, test, reinterpret, or later understand the deception when the scenario expects them to do so.
- High-consequence decisions are not reduced to guessing what the facilitator secretly decided.
- Ordinary information remains trustworthy enough that investigation can be selective rather than universal.
- Solving or learning a recurring deception pattern is allowed to improve later player decisions.
- Any direct facilitator deception produces a usable later payoff, consequence, or understanding rather than arbitrary invalidation of player reasoning.

## Notes
Misdirection changes interpretation rather than merely controlling access. It can be delivered through actors, environments, incomplete evidence, presentation, or direct facilitation, but its value comes from what players can do with the resulting uncertainty. Overuse can collapse the information economy: if everything may be false, checking everything becomes rational and pacing degrades. Keep deception purposeful, bounded, and compatible with player learning.
