# Run Consequential NPCs from Executable Social State

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an NPC can materially change information, access, alliances, obligations, danger, resources, or later events through social interaction
**THEN** represent the smallest current social state that lets the facilitator derive the NPC's next decision: goals, knowledge, relationships, leverage, constraints, default behavior, escalation boundaries, and state-change triggers
**ELSE** keep the NPC at the lighter representation appropriate to an incidental or one-function role.

## Do
- Record what the NPC currently wants and which competing duty, fear, attachment, dependency, or constraint limits how directly they can pursue it.
- Separate what the NPC knows from what the facilitator knows so responses follow available information rather than hidden author knowledge.
- Identify relationships that alter behavior, including trust, debt, loyalty, rivalry, authority, dependence, protection, resentment, or negotiated obligation when they matter to play.
- Give the NPC concrete leverage they can actually use, such as access, testimony, money, status, permission, expertise, contacts, resources, or the ability to withhold cooperation.
- State a default response for ordinary interaction and one or more thresholds that change it when pressure, evidence, betrayal, payment, danger, or prior treatment crosses a meaningful boundary.
- Update only the social state that play changes. A new promise, injury, discovery, humiliation, favor, loss, or alliance should alter later behavior when it changes the NPC's incentives or available options.
- Use the state to improvise unscripted dialogue and choices rather than predicting every possible player line or writing a branching conversation tree.
- Scale the representation to consequence. A recurring but simple contact may need only a motive, one leverage point, and one escalation boundary; a pivotal actor may require several competing pressures and relationships.

## Don't
- Replace playable social state with a personality paragraph that sounds vivid but does not tell the facilitator what the NPC will do under pressure.
- Script a full dialogue tree for every anticipated player approach.
- Let an NPC know facts they have no route to learn merely because the facilitator needs a preferred scene to happen.
- Treat loyalty, hostility, fear, or affection as immutable labels when play has materially changed the relationship.
- Give every incidental NPC the same social-state depth as an actor whose choices can redirect the adventure.
- Use a hidden predetermined outcome to override a state that clearly supports a different response.

## Checklist
- The NPC's current goal is explicit enough to predict what they pursue now.
- The NPC's knowledge is distinguishable from facilitator-only truth.
- At least one relationship, constraint, or leverage point explains how the NPC can affect play when the role is consequential.
- The facilitator can name the NPC's ordinary response and at least one condition that would change it when escalation matters.
- Persistent player treatment or changed circumstances can update the state without rewriting the whole NPC.
- An unscripted but plausible player approach can be answered from the stored state rather than from a guessed author intention.
- Representation depth matches the NPC's expected consequence rather than their raw mechanical power.

## Notes
A consequential social actor is easier to run when the adventure stores decisions rather than biography. Goals create direction; knowledge bounds what the actor can react to; relationships and leverage shape available moves; constraints and thresholds explain why behavior changes. This representation supports improvisation because the facilitator can derive a new response from current state instead of selecting from a prewritten branch.

This Pattern owns the **adventure-facing social state needed to derive one consequential NPC's choices**. Scaling mechanical representation, preparing the wider scenario as a toolbox, and defining campaign-scale faction resources remain separate decisions.
