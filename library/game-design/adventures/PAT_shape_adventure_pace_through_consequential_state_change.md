# Shape Adventure Pace Through Consequential State Change

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an adventure's intended experience depends on a particular rhythm of pressure, recovery, discovery, escalation, or decision
**THEN** shape momentum through the cadence and contrast of consequential changes in information, options, resources, relationships, risk, objectives, time, or world state rather than by maximizing raw activity
**ELSE** let the table set its own tempo without adding encounters, rolls, or complications merely to make the session feel busy.

## Do
- Identify which state changes actually move the current situation: new information, lost or gained options, resource shifts, relationship changes, altered risk, objective changes, deadlines, positional changes, or persistent world reactions.
- Vary pressure and recovery according to the intended experience. A pursuit, investigation, siege, social drama, survival journey, and exploratory crawl can all need different rhythms.
- Let fictional delay matter when the established situation supports it. Exposure, noise, fatigue, wandering threats, deadlines, patrols, resource use, or missed opportunities can turn hesitation into consequence without inventing arbitrary punishment.
- Distinguish activity from progress. Combat, travel procedure, dialogue, and repeated checks earn time when they create decisions or meaningful state change, not merely because they keep participants occupied.
- Compress transitions or repeated procedure when nothing important can change inside them, and expand moments where choices, uncertainty, or consequences deserve attention.
- Allow preparation and strong play to shorten a confrontation or bypass an expected beat when the accumulated state honestly supports that result.
- Test pacing in representative play when tempo matters. Authored structure can create pacing affordances, but actual table tempo also depends on participant behavior and facilitation.

## Don't
- Equate more combat rounds, dice rolls, or scene count with faster or better pacing.
- Add random encounters or complications solely as filler when they do not express time, risk, world activity, choice, or another concrete play function.
- Stretch a climax after the decisive uncertainty is gone merely to make it feel longer.
- Treat player socializing or table chatter itself as an in-fiction offense; only apply consequences that follow from fictional time, exposure, noise, or neglected precautions.
- Assume that a written sequence guarantees the same tempo for every group.
- Compress aftermath, travel, recovery, or conversation automatically when those moments contain decisions, relationship changes, consequences, or player-valued reflection.

## Checklist
- A representative segment has identifiable consequential state changes rather than only activity.
- Pressure and recovery serve the intended experience instead of following one default rhythm.
- Any cost for delay follows from established fictional conditions or procedures.
- Low-decision activity can be compressed without losing information, agency, or consequence.
- A fast resolution remains valid when players have earned it through preparation or prior state changes.
- When pacing is important to the design, actual play is used to verify tempo rather than relying only on author prediction.

## Notes
Adventure pace is an emergent property of both design and table behavior. A designer can control how often meaningful information, choices, risks, resources, relationships, and objectives are allowed to change, but cannot dictate how quickly a particular group will talk, deliberate, joke, investigate, or resolve procedures. Designing for consequential change creates usable pressure and recovery without confusing motion with progress.

Variant `game_design_variant_escalate_through_causal_setbacks_instead_of_steady_success` uses reversals as part of the adventure's pressure rhythm instead of assuming every intermediate beat should be a narrow victory. A useful setback changes what the players can protect, pursue, spend, trust, or attempt next: a costly win can consume resources needed for the larger goal, a missed deadline can close one opportunity while opening another problem, a defeated party can retreat and prepare against a previously overwhelming foe, or lost support can force a new route. Prefer setbacks that players can trace to an established choice, missed opportunity, opponent response, or known condition when the reversal is meant to feel earned. Pure bad luck can create short-term excitement, but it is a weak substitute for a consequential state change when the design is asking players to learn from or respond to failure.
