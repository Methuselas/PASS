# Use Persistent Fictional Assets as Rewards

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a campaign's rewards are dominated by disposable wealth, equipment, or one-time prizes and play would benefit from stronger attachment to the fictional world
**THEN** reward characters with durable fictional assets such as dependable relationships, community standing, property, a safe home, recurring services, or obligations that remain available for players to use, improve, and care about across later play
**ELSE** keep rewards compact when the game does not revisit people, places, or persistent world state often enough for those assets to matter

## Do
- Give the reward a recurring use or relationship: a trusted friend can provide help or information, a community can welcome and depend on the characters, and a home can support refuge, customization, and a meaningful return point.
- Let players deepen the asset through voluntary investment of time, money, attention, favors, gifts, improvements, or protection so attachment can grow from what they choose to put into it.
- When property is part of the reward, let upgrades such as locks, traps, hidden rooms, or specialist spaces absorb surplus wealth voluntarily while leaving players with a visible improvement rather than a confiscatory fee.
- Carry positive consequences forward when players contribute to a place or group; improved prosperity, changed routines, new services, recognition, or stronger relationships make the reward visibly persistent.
- Allow some allies and safe places to remain genuinely reliable. Occasional assistance or uncomplicated welcome can be part of the payoff rather than merely setup for a reversal.
- Reuse established people and places when later adventures need compatible functions so accumulated familiarity can do work that a generic replacement would have to rebuild.

## Don't
- Treat every valued NPC as a future betrayer, hostage, impostor, possession victim, or corpse merely because players have become attached to them.
- Destroy, loot, or revoke a home or community reward by default as soon as players invest in it; repeated punishment teaches players that attachment is strategically foolish.
- Declare that characters care about a town, family, ally, or property without giving players repeated opportunities to interact with, benefit from, or change it.
- Treat friends, family, community, or property only as bait for danger; a connection can be valuable because it remains supportive and worth returning to.
- Call a generic unnamed town, shop, or helper a meaningful connection without carrying forward the specific people, habits, services, or changes that made it familiar.

## Checklist
- At least one nonmaterial or place-based reward can recur in later play and provide a concrete benefit, relationship, choice, or source of identity.
- Players have a voluntary way to invest further in the asset and can observe at least one lasting consequence of doing so.
- Some established allies, homes, or communities can remain dependable long enough that trusting and investing in them is rational.
- The design does not rely on betrayal, kidnapping, destruction, or loss as the automatic next beat after attachment forms.
- Reusing the rewarded person or place later preserves prior state instead of resetting it to a generic service point.
- The reward still earns its place if no later scenario ever threatens it.

## Notes
Persistent rewards can make the game world itself part of what players earn. A familiar tavern, trusted local, adopted community, or player-owned home can accumulate value through return, recognition, practical support, and visible change without increasing combat power or handing out another pile of currency. The reward becomes stronger as players voluntarily invest in it and later play remembers that investment.

The same persistence that creates stake weight can be damaged by reflexively weaponizing every attachment. If friendship always becomes betrayal and property always becomes a target, players learn to avoid the very connections the campaign is trying to cultivate. Threatening an established asset can still be effective when the scenario genuinely calls for it; this rule only preserves the possibility that a valued connection may also remain a positive, reliable part of play.
