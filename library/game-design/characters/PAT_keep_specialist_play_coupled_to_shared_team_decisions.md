# Keep Specialist Play Coupled to Shared Team Decisions

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a specialist role gains privileged verbs, state, or procedures that can occupy extended live play
**THEN** couple that specialist activity to shared objectives and shared state so teammates can still make consequential decisions around it, and bound any interval that becomes an exclusive parallel game
**ELSE** allow short isolated specialist moments when their participation cost is deliberate and the rest of the table is not left without meaningful decisions for an unacceptable interval.

## Do
- Prefer specialist outputs that change teammate decision surfaces by opening routes, suppressing threats, exposing information, protecting allies, changing positioning, or altering another shared constraint.
- Let teammates alter the specialist's operating conditions through access, protection, credentials, positioning, signal quality, resources, or other shared state when the fiction supports it.
- Use shared platforms or shared objectives when they can preserve deep specialist control without moving the specialist into a separate game state for long periods.
- Keep the specialist's objective, progress, danger, and expected shared consequence legible enough that non-specialists can decide around it without learning the subsystem's internal jargon.
- Distinguish shared state from shared decisions; benefiting from the specialist's result does not by itself give teammates agency during the procedure.
- Prefer privileged control over shared state to ownership of many independently resolving actors when both express the same fantasy.
- When exclusive environments are intrinsic to the fantasy, bound their live duration, alternate attention, or provide explicit cross-domain decisions rather than assuming later spotlight compensation repairs the local waiting interval.

## Don't
- Protect a specialist niche by making the rest of the party unable to participate in the shared objective.
- Call a subsystem integrated merely because its effects matter to everyone while only one player makes consequential decisions during live resolution.
- Treat synchronized initiative, shared narration, or visible screens as equivalent to shared agency.
- Fix specialist under-participation by giving the specialist several autonomous actors whose independent turns consume even more table bandwidth.
- Count later spotlight compensation as a reduction in the waiting already created by an isolated live procedure.

## Checklist
- At least one specialist success creates or changes a consequential decision for another participant.
- At least one representative scene tests whether non-specialists can improve the specialist's access or operating conditions.
- Non-specialists can understand the current objective, progress, and danger well enough to make informed decisions around the specialist activity.
- Shared-state coupling and shared-decision coupling can be distinguished in the tested procedure.
- The specialist's strongest mode does not silently turn into a long exclusive parallel game or a stream of independently resolving subordinate actors.
- Any intentionally isolated specialist segment has a bounded participation cost that the campaign structure can support.

## Notes
A specialist can be distinctive without becoming a separate game. The strongest architecture is often bidirectional: the specialist changes shared state for teammates while teammates can materially change the specialist's access, risk, or available targets. Shared platforms such as vehicles, defenses, sensor networks, or command structures often make that coupling natural. Exclusive digital, astral, scouting, or remote environments require more deliberate attention management because synchronization and shared consequences do not guarantee shared decisions. Participation is a live-play property: shortening a later scene does not refund the waiting already created by an isolated procedure.
