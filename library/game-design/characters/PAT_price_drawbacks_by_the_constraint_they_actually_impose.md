# Price Drawbacks by the Constraint They Actually Impose

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a drawback reduces build cost or grants positive build currency
**THEN** value it by how often, how severely, and how unavoidably it removes choices, access, reliability, resources, or other useful capability in the intended campaign ecology
**ELSE** do not award economic value to a nominal flaw that does not create a meaningful constraint in play.

## Do
- Measure trigger frequency, severity, controllability, recovery difficulty, social exposure, lost choices, and how easily the affected domain can be avoided.
- Test the same drawback on materially different builds when role, equipment, campaign focus, or other capabilities can change how restrictive it is.
- If drawbacks refund a fungible build currency, compare builds that deliberately fill the allowed negative budget with builds that do not.
- Treat pressure to fill the maximum drawback allowance as an economy-level incentive even when every individual drawback is technically legal.
- Reprice or remove a drawback's refund when another broadly useful purchase, campaign condition, or advancement state reliably neutralizes the meaningful constraint.
- Distinguish a colorful inconvenience from a constraint that repeatedly changes decisions.
- Re-evaluate drawbacks when campaign ecology changes enough that the affected domain becomes much more or less relevant.

## Don't
- Assume a disadvantage is valuable because its label sounds severe when its trigger is rare, avoidable, irrelevant to the build, or easily resisted.
- Treat a cap on negative points as proof that drawback incentives are solved; a cap limits magnitude but can still make filling it the rational default.
- Let a general-purpose purchase erase a drawback while preserving the full refund that drawback financed.
- Price every drawback as if every character and campaign values the affected capability equally.
- Reward flaws that the character can routinely route around without sacrificing anything the intended play actually values.

## Checklist
- Each priced drawback names the concrete choices, access, resources, reliability, or opportunities it removes.
- Frequency, severity, controllability, and avoidability have been tested rather than inferred from the trait's fiction alone.
- At least two materially different builds are compared when drawback value could be role-dependent.
- If drawbacks fund positive abilities, the economy has been tested for pressure to fill the allowed negative budget.
- A purchase or campaign condition that neutralizes the drawback also changes its economic value when appropriate.
- The drawback still constrains actual play in the campaign ecology used to justify its price.

## Notes
A drawback is economically valuable only while it actually costs the character something the game cares about. Fictionally dramatic labels can hide weak constraints, and a hard cap on refunded points can hide a different incentive problem: if every negative point buys positive capability, rational players may feel pushed to fill the cap regardless of concept. The useful pricing question is not **"How bad does this sound?"** but **"How often does this remove a meaningful option, and how difficult is that cost to avoid or neutralize?"**
