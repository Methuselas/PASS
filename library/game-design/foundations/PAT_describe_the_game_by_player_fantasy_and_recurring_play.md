# Describe the Game by Player Fantasy and Recurring Play

## Pattern Rule
**IF** the game must be described to prospective players, testers, collaborators, or buyers
**THEN** connect enough world or genre context to establish the player fantasy with a truthful account of what players repeatedly do and the meaningful experience or structure that distinguishes the game
**ELSE** do not rely on a genre label, lore summary, or novelty claim when the audience does not need external positioning.

## Do
- State who the game is for in terms consistent with the intended-player definition rather than treating broad reach as proof that everyone is the target.
- Give enough world or genre context to make the player role and fantasy legible.
- Describe recurring play in action terms: what players investigate, negotiate, build, fight, manage, travel through, risk, or otherwise spend table time doing.
- State meaningful distinctions in terms of experience, structure, mechanics, setting interaction, or their combination rather than novelty for novelty's sake.
- Check the description against observed or executable play so promotion does not promise behavior the rules do not reliably produce.
- Use the intended-audience definition to choose language and communication contexts the likely players can understand and encounter without changing the design promise to chase every reached audience.

## Don't
- Describe a game only by lore when prospective players still cannot tell what they will do.
- Describe a game only by mechanics when the player fantasy or setting relationship is necessary to understand why those mechanics matter.
- Market a play experience that the rules do not reliably produce.
- Treat familiar genre imagery as a substitute for explaining the game's recurring activity.
- Let promotional reach retroactively redefine the audience the design was built to serve.

## Checklist
- The description identifies the player fantasy or role, recurring play, and at least one meaningful distinction.
- Genre or setting context explains the experience rather than standing in for it.
- Claims about recurring activity can be traced to actual procedures, scenarios, or observed play.
- The intended audience can be named without requiring the entire rulebook as context.
- Reached audience, actual players, and intended audience remain distinguishable populations.

## Notes
External communication is downstream of the design promise. A genre label can create useful expectations, but it cannot explain which part of that genre the game makes playable or how the table spends its time. A useful description therefore joins fiction and operation: who the players get to be, what they repeatedly do, and why this implementation is meaningfully different. The communication should stay truthful to the intended audience and the game's actual behavior rather than becoming a second design process driven by whoever happens to encounter the promotion.
