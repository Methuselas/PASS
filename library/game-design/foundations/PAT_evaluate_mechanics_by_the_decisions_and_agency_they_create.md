# Evaluate Mechanics by the Decisions and Agency They Create

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a mechanic is being evaluated for the kind of player agency and uncertainty it creates
**THEN** map the meaningful decisions available before, during, and after resolution, identify where outcome control comes from, and judge whether that distribution of control supports the intended experience
**ELSE** do not judge the mechanic only by whether its arithmetic or procedure resolves correctly.

## Do
- Separate the mechanic's calculation from the decisions that feed it and the choices that follow its result.
- Identify where players can spend resources, accept risk, change position, choose timing, push a result, prepare, cooperate, or otherwise influence consequences.
- Distinguish control supplied by player choice from control supplied by character capability, chance, facilitator judgment, hidden procedure, or automatic state.
- Check whether the intended game wants high player control, high uncertainty, facilitator judgment, or a deliberate mixture rather than treating one distribution as universally superior.
- Test whether apparently different choices materially change consequences, timing, exposure, resources, information, or future opportunity; cosmetic branches do not create meaningful agency.
- For adverse outcomes, make the causal path legible enough that players can understand why the result occurred and can judge the procedure as legitimate even when they dislike the outcome.
- When players can alter a bad result, attach the intervention to scarcity, opportunity cost, added risk, changed position, or another meaningful consequence so the second chance remains a decision rather than an automatic erasure.

## Don't
- Equate more listed options with more agency when the options do not materially change outcomes or future play.
- Treat randomness as automatically hostile to agency; uncertainty can support an experience when players make consequential choices around it.
- Treat determinism as automatically empowering when the relevant result is fixed by build choices, hidden state, or facilitator fiat rather than an active player decision.
- Call a result fair merely because its probabilities are mathematically consistent when players cannot understand the causal path or make the kinds of choices the game promises.
- Let a second-chance mechanic routinely erase undesirable results without asking the player to spend, risk, or choose anything meaningful.
- Count arithmetic, lookup, bookkeeping, or implementation detail as player agency merely because the player performs it; operating cost is a separate design question.

## Checklist
- The mechanic's meaningful decision points before, during, and after resolution can be named.
- The sources of outcome control and uncertainty are explicit.
- Player choices materially change consequences in ways appropriate to the intended experience.
- Apparent options that do not change consequences are not counted as meaningful agency.
- Failure and other adverse results have a causal path players can understand well enough to judge the procedure as legitimate.
- Any second-chance rule preserves uncertainty by attaching a meaningful choice, cost, risk, or scarcity to intervention.

## Notes
A mechanic does more than produce an answer: it distributes control. The same probability can feel very different depending on whether players choose when to risk it, can spend scarce resources around it, understand what made failure likely, or merely watch the procedure decide for them. Agency is therefore not a synonym for certainty or for number of options. It is the player's ability to make consequential choices within the uncertainty profile the game intends. Operating burden, reward alignment, playtest evidence classification, and revision validation are separate decisions with their own owners; this Pattern stays focused on what the mechanic lets players decide and how that control shapes the experience.
