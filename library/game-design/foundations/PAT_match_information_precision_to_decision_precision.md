# Match Information Precision to Decision Precision

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a meaningful decision depends on a distance, duration, capacity, size, quantity, timing, position, visibility state, or other measurable fact
**THEN** present that fact at enough precision for the player and facilitator to evaluate the choice under the game's rules
**ELSE** use approximate language when additional precision would not change play.

## Do
- Start with the coarsest description that supports the actual decision.
- Increase precision when movement, range, timing, capacity, cover, visibility, positioning, resource limits, or another rule makes the distinction consequential.
- Use categories, bands, exact values, or another representation that matches the resolution grammar rather than adding numerical detail for its own sake.
- Make the needed precision available before the player commits to the decision it informs.
- Keep descriptive prose and mechanical representation compatible so the table does not have to guess which one controls.

## Don't
- Use vague terms such as "nearby," "large," or "far away" when the rules require a more precise distinction to evaluate a consequential choice.
- Add exact measurements merely for realism when no decision changes at that precision.
- Force players to request basic scale repeatedly when the scene's supported choices obviously depend on it.
- Provide more decimal or unit detail than the rules can actually use.

## Checklist
- Every mechanically consequential quantity is stated at a precision the relevant rule can consume.
- Approximate descriptions remain approximate where exact values do not change decisions.
- Players receive decision-relevant precision before committing to the choice.
- The chosen representation is no finer than the game's actual resolution needs.
- Fictional description and mechanical scale do not contradict each other.

## Notes
Information precision is a decision-support problem, not a realism contest. A "large hangar" can be enough orientation until a player must decide whether a movement rate, weapon range, spell, flight distance, or line of sight reaches across it. At that point missing scale becomes missing play information. Conversely, an exact measurement adds operating burden without value when every supported choice treats the space the same way. Match the representation to the distinction the rules and decisions actually need.
