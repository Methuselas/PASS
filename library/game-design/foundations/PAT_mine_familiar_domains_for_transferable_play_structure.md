# Mine Familiar Domains for Transferable Play Structure

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a game needs grounded roles, exchanges, institutions, routines, resources, or pressures and the designer deeply understands a real, historical, occupational, or hobby domain
**THEN** abstract the operating relationships that make that familiar domain function, transfer the useful functions into player-facing game structures, and rebuild them for the destination game's decisions, scale, and incentives
**ELSE** use another source of design structure when familiarity would contribute only theme or trivia

## Do
- Identify what actually moves through the familiar domain: goods, information, money, authority, labor, risk, favors, status, access, or other resources.
- Map the recurring roles and dependencies that create activity, such as suppliers, brokers, specialists, clients, protectors, rivals, regulators, or support services.
- Ask which relationships can become repeated player decisions, opportunities, constraints, or return loops rather than importing the domain only as setting color.
- Translate the function rather than the surface form: preserve the useful dependency or exchange while changing names, technology, fiction, scale, and consequences to fit the game.
- Test the transferred structure against the destination game's pacing, agency, incentives, and resource assumptions before treating it as a stable subsystem or scenario frame.
- Keep only enough real-domain detail to make the resulting play structure legible and internally coherent.

## Don't
- Assume a real institution or practice is automatically good game design because it functions in reality.
- Copy historical or occupational surface detail without identifying the player-facing decisions it is meant to create.
- Preserve realism at the expense of the destination game's pace, agency, balance, or intended experience.
- Use personal expertise as authority to add procedures players never interact with.
- Treat analogy as proof that two domains have identical incentives or constraints.

## Checklist
- The source domain can be described in terms of roles, exchanges, routines, resources, and pressures rather than only names or aesthetics.
- At least one transferred relationship creates a recurring player-facing decision, service, opportunity, or constraint.
- The destination implementation has been rebuilt for its own scale and rules rather than copied literally.
- Real-world detail that does not affect play can be removed without breaking the transferred structure.
- The resulting structure still fits the game's intended pacing, agency, incentives, and resource economy.
- The design remains understandable to players who do not share the designer's source-domain expertise.

## Notes
Familiarity is useful when it provides causal structure, not merely flavor. A designer who knows a trade, institution, historical practice, workplace, hobby, or other field can often see relationships that generic invention misses: who supplies whom, where information travels, what services cluster together, which resources are scarce, and what pressures make people return.

The transfer is an act of redesign. A brokerage model, supply chain, guild practice, expedition economy, or other familiar structure can inspire game activity, but the destination needs its own stakes, timescale, incentives, and level of abstraction. Use the source domain to discover workable relationships, then judge the implementation by the play it creates rather than by fidelity to the original model.
