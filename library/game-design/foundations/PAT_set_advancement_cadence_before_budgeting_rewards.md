# Set Advancement Cadence Before Budgeting Rewards

## Pattern Rule
**IF** the game intends a roughly predictable amount of successful play between persistent advancement steps
**THEN** choose that target cadence first, calculate the reward volume needed to support it, and distribute that budget through the behaviors and reward channels the game is meant to encourage
**ELSE** let advancement pace emerge from play when variability in progression is itself part of the intended campaign experience.

## Do
- Express cadence in a table-usable unit such as successful sessions, adventures, missions, milestones, or campaign cycles.
- Project the advancement cost for representative characters and work backward to the expected reward volume per cycle.
- Divide the budget among combat, exploration, objectives, treasure, relationships, discovery, or other reward sources according to the behavior the game wants repeated.
- Recheck the target against actual play frequency; the same number of adventures per advancement can feel radically different in a weekly versus quarterly campaign.
- Compare observed advancement to the target across several cycles before changing the reward budget, and distinguish bad luck from a structural pacing error.
- Retune the delivery mechanism when larger reward requirements make the old physical or fictional form of rewards cumbersome.

## Don't
- Decide reward quantities independently in every encounter when the campaign promises a controlled advancement pace.
- Retroactively invent enough reward to force every individual session onto schedule.
- Use cadence planning to reward behavior that contradicts the game's stated objectives merely because that behavior is easy to quantify.
- Treat one character's advancement cost as representative when roles progress at materially different rates.

## Checklist
- The intended amount of successful play between advancement steps is stated.
- Representative advancement costs have been translated into an expected reward budget per cycle.
- The reward mix reinforces the behaviors the game intends players to repeat.
- Actual play is compared against the target over enough cycles to distinguish noise from drift.
- The design states whether pacing is a target to calibrate or an outcome intentionally left emergent.

## Notes
Reward design has two separable questions: what deserves a reward, and how much reward should enter the campaign over time. A game can align rewards perfectly with intended behavior and still advance far faster or slower than intended. Setting a cadence makes that second variable testable. It should remain a planning target rather than a guarantee; unexpected risk, failure, player choice, and campaign interruption may still move individual groups away from the projection.
