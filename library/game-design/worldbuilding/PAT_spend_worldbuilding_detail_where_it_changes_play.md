# Spend Worldbuilding Detail Where It Changes Play

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a setting element is being developed for a playable world
**THEN** spend detail on the parts players need to understand, encounter, decide around, or use during play
**ELSE** leave low-impact areas lightly sketched until play creates a reason to develop them.

## Do
- Ask how the element reaches the characters through daily life, institutions, travel, resources, customs, conflict, sensory experience, or adventure opportunities.
- Develop institutions in proportion to their relevance: a divine-magic game may need churches in detail, while an educational system may need little attention unless schooling matters to play.
- Use memorable, interactable details such as greetings, clothing, food, phrases, holidays, local practices, and unusual modes of travel to make places distinct at the table.
- Revisit the setting as character creation, abilities, and adventure structure reveal which parts of the world need more support.
- For adventure backstory, work backward from current playable state: develop enough history to explain why the present actors, evidence, locations, resources, constraints, relationships, and pressures are here, then stop until another current decision requires more.
- Keep rich lore when it buys recognition, atmosphere, adventure material, or usable referee context; economy of detail is not a demand for a sparse setting.

## Don't
- Exhaustively describe ordinary infrastructure merely because it exists in a real society when the game gives players no meaningful reason to interact with it.
- Treat page count or encyclopedic coverage as proof that a world will feel immersive in play.
- Spend the same design effort on every culture, institution, region, and daily-life detail regardless of its effect on the intended experience.
- Write long historical chains first and then search for a playable problem that justifies them when the adventure only needs a few causal facts to explain its current situation.

## Checklist
- Each heavily developed setting element has a visible route into player understanding, choice, atmosphere, adventure, or referee use.
- Institutions central to character abilities or recurring adventures receive enough detail to operate coherently.
- At least some memorable details can be encountered or used rather than only read about.
- Rich lore earns its space through recognition, atmosphere, adventure utility, or referee usability rather than volume alone.
- Adventure backstory can be traced forward into at least one current actor, clue, location, resource, pressure, or decision rather than existing only as explanatory history.

## Notes
Worldbuilding becomes useful when players can inhabit it rather than merely read it. A richly documented setting and a compact scaffold can both work when their detail changes what characters understand, experience, or do. The appropriate information budget depends on how often a setting element reaches play and how much value that contact creates. Adventure backstory is especially efficient when it is derived from the present situation instead of written as an independent chronicle: the hidden harbor exists because the current smuggling operation needs a route, the trade relationship exists because it explains the contraband supply, and those facts immediately create investigative interfaces. More history can be added when play actually exposes a need for it. Encounter-level fictional grounding and preservation of deliberate negative space are separate decisions with their own owners.
