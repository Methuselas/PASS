# Stage High-Pressure Choices with Conflicting Evidence

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an adventure scene should force a consequential judgment before the characters can fully resolve uncertainty
**THEN** give the operator the actual situation, present the players with at least two plausible interpretations supported by observable but conflicting evidence, add credible time or authority pressure, and write consequences for the major responses without hiding every discriminating clue until after the choice
**ELSE** state the situation directly when ambiguity would add only delay, guesswork, or a gotcha

## Do
- Write the operator-facing truth first: what is actually happening, what each apparent threat or signal means, and which facts remain genuinely unknown even to the operator.
- Separate observable evidence from conclusions voiced by authorities, frightened witnesses, antagonists, or the characters' own assumptions.
- Give more than one interpretation enough support that a reasonable person could act on it under pressure.
- Put at least one potentially discriminating clue in reach before an irreversible action, even if noticing or trusting it requires attention, restraint, investigation, or defying a mistaken assumption.
- Name the pressure that prevents perfect certainty, such as an approaching deadline, vulnerable bystanders, an order, a fleeing suspect, a countdown, or rapidly changing conditions.
- Write the immediate and surviving consequences of the major response families so restraint, compliance, escalation, delay, and improvisation do not all collapse into the same outcome.
- When the truth is revealed later, use it to reinterpret the earlier evidence and consequences rather than claiming that the players were foolish for lacking information the module never made available.
- If the adventure repeats this kind of judgment, vary what is misleading, what is at risk, and which response is actually safest so the technique does not become a predictable trick.

## Don't
- Make every alarming cue secretly harmless; repeated reversals teach players that the safest response is to ignore the fiction.
- Demand a split-second decision and then reveal that the only useful evidence was inaccessible until afterward.
- Treat an authority's certainty as objective truth in the operator text when that authority is meant to be mistaken.
- Punish a reasonable choice solely because the author preferred another answer.
- Make all branches reconverge immediately with no lasting difference after presenting the decision as morally or operationally significant.
- Hide the actual situation from the operator so they cannot answer unanticipated questions consistently.

## Checklist
- The operator can state the actual situation separately from every in-world interpretation of it.
- At least two plausible readings are supported by evidence available before the decision.
- At least one clue can distinguish or complicate those readings before irreversible action.
- A named pressure explains why the characters cannot simply wait for complete certainty.
- The module gives runnable consequences for the major likely responses.
- The later reveal remains compatible with the evidence that was actually available earlier.
- Repeated ambiguity scenes do not all reward the same reflex or rely on the same reversal.

## Notes
A pressured choice becomes meaningful when uncertainty is part of the situation rather than a missing paragraph in the module. The writer should know the truth, the operator should know which signals are reliable or misleading, and the players should receive enough contradictory evidence to exercise judgment under constrained time. Orders, alarms, apparent weapons, panicked crowds, suspicious behavior, and incomplete transmissions can all create pressure, but the scene remains fair only when the fiction contains something that careful players could use before the consequence becomes irreversible.

This structure is especially useful when an adventure wants to test obedience, restraint, threat identification, trust, or interpretation. It should not be used as a universal difficulty dial. If the scene's real purpose is simply to communicate the next objective, ambiguity adds friction without adding a decision.
