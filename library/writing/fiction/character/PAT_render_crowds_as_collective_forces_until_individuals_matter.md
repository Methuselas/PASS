# Render Crowds as Collective Forces until Individuals Matter

## Pattern Rule
**IF** a fiction scene contains more people than the reader needs to track individually and the group's importance comes mainly from its scale, movement, pressure, or shared action
**THEN** render the crowd first as a collective force through coordinated behavior, spatial consequence, and a few exact details, while individually tracking only people whose actions change orientation, choice, relationship, or consequence
**ELSE** distinguish the relevant individuals or subgroups when their differences are what the scene is about

## Do
- Establish enough of the crowd's scale and disposition early for the reader to understand the pressure it creates and for character choices about approach, retreat, concealment, or confrontation to feel informed.
- Show what the group does as a group: it can close a street, surge toward a doorway, break around an obstacle, chant, scatter, compress personal space, block sightlines, or redirect movement.
- Use a small number of concrete sights, sounds, motions, or repeated objects to make the mass perceptible without itemizing every member.
- Keep ambient members collective or role-based until one person becomes causally important; then promote that person into individual tracking with enough orientation to follow the change.
- Shift from collective to individual or subgroup rendering when the scene stops turning on mass behavior and begins turning on particular agents.
- Re-establish the collective effect after a focal exchange so the scene does not shrink accidentally from a crowd event into an isolated conversation.

## Don't
- Introduce, name, or visually distinguish every person merely because the writer can see the whole crowd.
- Substitute a head count for experienced scale; numbers alone do not show formation, density, motion, sound, or what the group prevents characters from doing.
- Hide the group's operative scale until after a character has made a choice that should reasonably depend on seeing or sensing that scale, unless the viewpoint itself cannot know it.
- Keep describing background members after their details stop changing pressure, orientation, atmosphere, or consequence.
- Keep the crowd in collective mode after the scene has shifted to particular people whose different actions now determine what happens.

## Checklist
- A reader can tell what the crowd changes about movement, risk, attention, access, or available action.
- The scene conveys scale through collective behavior and selected concrete detail rather than a cast list or abstract number alone.
- Individually tracked people earn that attention through consequence.
- The prose changes granularity when particular people, rather than aggregate behavior, become consequential.
- A person who emerges from the crowd can be followed without forcing the reader to memorize everyone present.
- After close-focus exchanges, the continuing pressure of the larger group remains legible when it still matters.

## Notes
Large groups create a tracking problem different from an ordinary cast problem. The reader often needs to experience the crowd's aggregate force without converting dozens of people into dozens of characters. Collective verbs, spatial effects, repeated sensory cues, and a few consequential individuals let the prose preserve scale while controlling cognitive load. The mode can change inside one scene: a mass may first function as environment or pressure, then split into subgroups, then yield one individually consequential person. Collective rendering is therefore a compression strategy, not a claim that the people inside the group are interchangeable.
