# Calibrate Rhetorical Maturity and Cognitive Load to Reader Voice and Task

## Pattern Rule
**IF** revision advice would make a piece more sophisticated, compressed, explicit, polished, or structurally invisible
**THEN** first identify the intended reader, the writer/speaker/character whose maturity the prose should plausibly express, the task being performed, and the kind of difficulty the reader is being asked to carry; adjust complexity and visible scaffolding to those conditions rather than treating maximum sophistication as the goal
**ELSE** preserve the existing level when it is already appropriate to the reader, voice, task, and medium

## Do
- Separate **cognitive complexity** from **rhetorical maturity**: a simple surface can carry difficult ideas, and an expert writer can make demanding material accessible without making it childish.
- Separate both from **visible scaffolding**: explicit topic sentences, repeated framing, first/second/third organization, direct explanation, and simple transitions may be useful at one developmental or instructional level and clumsy at another.
- Decide how much lexical difficulty, clause depth, abstraction, assumed knowledge, inference, and structural compression the intended reader can productively carry at once.
- Let the actual writer, narrator, speaker, or character determine the plausible level of articulation. Preserve how that person reasons, categorizes, associates, explains, and misinterprets rather than merely lowering vocabulary.
- Use concrete action, repetition, examples, narrator guidance, visual support, or other orientation when they allow readers to handle sophisticated material without unnecessary confusion.
- When editing a developing writer, distinguish genuine error or weakness from age-, experience-, or task-appropriate scaffolding before replacing it with a more expert rhetorical move.
- Let sophisticated craft live beneath an accessible surface when the audience needs it: causal design, motif, irony, rhythm, subtext, and structural payoff do not require elevated diction.
- Choose grammatical person for the work it performs in the genre and task, not because first person is inherently less professional or third person inherently more mature.

## Don't
- Equate writing for younger, novice, broad, or time-pressured readers with uniformly short sentences or simple ideas.
- Simulate a younger or less experienced writer by inserting random mistakes, misspellings, broken grammar, or artificial ignorance.
- "Improve" a child, novice, or character voice into adult professional prose merely because an experienced writer would hide the scaffolding.
- Force professional or academic prose to retain elementary signposting when the argument is already legible through stronger transitions and cumulative structure.
- Treat density, abstraction, advanced vocabulary, subtle transitions, or hidden structure as inherently superior forms of writing.
- Assume the same rhetorical maturity is appropriate merely because two works share the same intended reader age.
- Remove first person merely to make prose sound more academic, or repeat `I think` / `I liked` after the writer's stance is already established and the analysis can demonstrate it.

## Checklist
- The intended reader, writer/speaker/character, task, and medium can each be named.
- The prose asks readers to carry only the kinds of difficulty the piece has chosen deliberately.
- Vocabulary, syntax, abstraction, explanation, and paragraph architecture fit the rhetorical maturity being represented.
- Visible scaffolding is retained, reduced, or hidden because of function rather than prestige.
- A simpler surface does not erase conceptual depth, causal structure, voice, or thematic intelligence.
- A more sophisticated surface does not exceed the plausible articulation of the writer, narrator, or character.
- Revision has not mistaken "what I would write" for "what this writer/voice/task should sound like."

## Notes
Audience calibration is not a one-dimensional readability slider. Judy Blume and Lewis Carroll both write successfully for children through markedly different surfaces: direct contemporary simplicity in one case, and long sentences, parenthetical play, parody, verbal puzzles, and layered satire in the other. Accessibility depends on where the difficulty lives and what support the reader receives.

Quality and rhetorical maturity are separate variables. A developing or deliberately plain voice can carry strong causal reasoning, nuanced interpretation, and structural control through visible scaffolding; a professional voice may hide the same support inside cumulative transitions and denser syntax. Judge whether the surface fits its speaker, reader, and task, not whether it resembles the most polished available register.

When editing, ask: **Is this passage weak because it is poorly executed, or merely less rhetorically mature because that level is appropriate here?** The answer determines whether to teach, preserve, simplify, deepen, or professionalize the prose.


`writing_variant_choose_grammatical_person_by_function_not_prestige` separates grammatical person from rhetorical maturity. A first-person narrator may be formally sophisticated, technically exact, unreliable, intimate, reflective, comic, or analytically rigorous. Conversely, third-person prose can be simplistic or evasive. The relevant question is what access and responsibility the chosen person creates. In analytical or technical prose, first person often works best when it performs a specific act—stating an evaluation, method, limitation, or situated observation—then yields to evidence rather than becoming repetitive self-report.
