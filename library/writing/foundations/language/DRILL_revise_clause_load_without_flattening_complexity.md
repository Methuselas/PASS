# Revise Clause Load without Flattening Complexity

## Practice Task
Revise a dense passage so each sentence carries a deliberate rhetorical job while preserving the argument's qualifications, causal links, and conceptual depth.

## Target Skill
Control clause load and sentence boundaries so complex thinking remains precise without forcing the reader to unpack several major conceptual turns inside one syntactic container.

## Setup
Choose 250 to 600 words of finished analytical, reflective, explanatory, or argumentative prose that contains at least five sentences with multiple clauses, parenthetical qualifications, linked abstractions, compressed inferential steps, or several rhetorical jobs. Do not exclude a sentence merely because it is under 30 words. Keep the original version beside the revision.

## Instructions
1. Mark every sentence containing three or more clauses, more than one major conceptual turn, a chain of abstract nouns that hides the acting relationship, or an inferential bridge the reader must silently reconstruct.
2. For each marked sentence, state its dominant rhetorical job in a short phrase such as claim, evidence, qualification, contrast, causal link, synthesis, or transition.
3. Identify any additional major job the sentence is also trying to perform and record it separately.
4. Produce one revision of each overloaded sentence by splitting, reordering, subordinating, or redistributing material across neighboring sentences. Preserve every qualification or causal relation that the argument still needs.
5. Produce a second revision for at least two sentences that deliberately keeps the long syntax but improves hierarchy through word order and punctuation.
6. Compare the original and both revision routes aloud. Record which version you retain and why its sentence boundary or hierarchy better serves meaning and rhythm.
7. Re-read the whole passage and repair transitions created by any split so the revision does not become a sequence of disconnected short statements.
8. Inspect each run of three to five sentences for reader orientation and release. Where every sentence carries substantial conceptual weight, test one short declarative sentence that states the governing point, consequence, or turn. Keep it only if it improves rhythm and hierarchy rather than creating mechanical alternation.
9. Identify one sentence that remains long on purpose and name the effect its sustained syntax performs better than a split version would.

## Success Check
- Every sentence with three or more clauses or multiple major conceptual turns is marked and given one dominant rhetorical job.
- Moderate-length sentences are also inspected for compressed abstraction, hidden inferential bridges, and multiple rhetorical jobs; a low word count does not automatically pass the sentence.
- Additional major jobs are identified separately rather than being relabeled as minor details to avoid revising the sentence.
- Every marked sentence receives a real split, reorder, subordination, or redistribution test; simply deleting qualifiers does not count as reducing clause load.
- At least two dense sentences are also tested in a retained-long form, so the exercise does not assume that shorter is automatically better.
- The retained revisions preserve necessary qualifications and causal relations; a smoother sentence that changes the argument fails the drill.
- The revised passage contains repaired transitions, and the reader can state how each major sentence advances the argument without reconstructing hidden links.
- At least one dense multi-sentence stretch is tested for rhythmic release through a short orienting sentence, without requiring that the short version be retained.
- At least one long sentence is retained for a named rhetorical effect. If every long sentence is split by rule, the exercise demonstrates compression preference rather than controlled sentence design.

## Common Failures
- Counting words instead of identifying conceptual and clause structure.
- Treating a lower average or maximum sentence length as proof that density is solved.
- Replacing clauses with abstract noun phrases so the sentence becomes shorter but cognitively denser.
- Splitting syntax while leaving the same number of unstated inferential jumps packed into adjacent sentences.
- Splitting a sentence at every conjunction and producing choppy, disconnected prose.
- Improving readability by deleting the qualification that made the claim accurate.
- Keeping an overloaded sentence merely because it is grammatical.
- Treating punctuation as a substitute for deciding which idea is dominant.
- Declaring all long syntax sophisticated and all short syntax simplistic.

## Notes
The goal is hierarchy, not minimalism. Dense prose becomes difficult when several claims, qualifications, examples, abstractions, and causal turns compete for equal syntactic weight. Training evidence also shows that conceptual density can survive substantial reductions in sentence length: a sentence under 30 words may still ask the reader to resolve several unstated relationships before receiving orientation. A sentence can remain long when the reader can feel which thought governs the others and when duration itself serves the material. The useful revision question is not "Can this be shorter?" but "How many major jobs is this sentence asking the reader to complete before receiving a full stop, and does that burden create an effect worth keeping?"

`writing_variant_diagnose_conceptual_density_below_length_threshold` is the recovery route when a passage has already been shortened but still feels cognitively packed. It shifts the diagnostic from word count to conceptual burden: abstract compression, unstated bridges, competing rhetorical jobs, and the absence of rhythmic release.
