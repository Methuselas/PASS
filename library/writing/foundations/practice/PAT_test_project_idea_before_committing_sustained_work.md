# Test a Project Idea Before Committing Sustained Work

## Pattern Rule
**IF** an exciting premise would require substantial time and attention before it becomes a finished work
**THEN** capture the spark, develop it only far enough to expose its recurring questions and likely workload, and compare sustained interest with available capacity before committing
**ELSE** move into composition when the project is already fixed by an assignment, contract, deadline, or deliberate prior commitment

## Do
- Capture the premise and the specific element that makes it compelling before the initial wording or image fades.
- Revisit the idea after the first burst of novelty and notice whether it continues to generate questions, scenes, structures, images, or consequences worth pursuing.
- Sketch enough of an outline, governing elements, or likely research and revision burden to reveal work hidden by the attractive premise.
- Compare that likely investment with the time, deadline, attention, and competing projects actually available.
- Commit when both the material and the workload still justify sustained attention; preserve promising unchosen ideas in a retrievable form rather than developing all of them at once.
- After commitment, route an ordinary loss of excitement to the writing-practice and revision decisions instead of reopening project selection every difficult session.

## Don't
- Treat a vivid first idea as proof that the complete project will remain worth the required work.
- Demand a full draft or exhaustive outline merely to decide whether a candidate deserves commitment.
- Keep switching to newer ideas whenever the current project reaches its less exciting work.
- Use enthusiasm alone as a completion method after a deadline or deliberate commitment has already fixed the project.
- Assume that preserving an idea obligates the writer to develop it now.

## Checklist
- The initial spark and its strongest live element have been captured.
- The idea has survived at least one return beyond the first moment of excitement and still produces usable questions or material.
- A light outline or equivalent probe has exposed enough of the likely work to make scope visible.
- Available time and attention have been compared with that scope and with other active commitments.
- The project has an explicit commit, defer, or decline decision rather than remaining indefinitely half-started.
- Once committed, normal fatigue is handled as a process problem rather than mistaken automatically for evidence that the original selection was wrong.

## Notes
Early attraction and durable commitment are different tests. A premise can be worth capturing yet not worth the hours needed to research, draft, revise, and finish it now. Conversely, a project that survives an initial scope probe and keeps generating consequential possibilities has earned more than momentary enthusiasm. Make the selection decision before large investment when possible; once the work is deliberately underway, use `writing_build_sustainable_writing_practice_around_real_constraints` for ordinary fluctuations in energy rather than continually auditioning replacement projects.
